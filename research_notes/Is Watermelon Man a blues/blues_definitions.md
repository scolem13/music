# Definitions of "a blues": form, harmony, melody, genre; status of 8-/16-bar and blues-derived tunes

Research limits: paywalled/print sources (Grove, Levine, Coker, Aebersold, Kubik, Evans, Oliver, Titon, Schuller, Murray, Baraka) were NOT directly accessible; claims about them are only as reported by the secondary/open sources cited. Items from memory only are listed under Gaps as leads, not findings.

## 1. Standard definitions (form, harmony, melody/scale, lyric)

### Takeaway
Reference/pedagogical works treat the 12-bar, I-IV-V, dominant-7th, AAB, blue-note package as the prototype. Scholars note it is a standardization (commercial recording era) and not a universal criterion; early blues had no single structure.

### Cited Findings
- Blues is described as both a genre and a form; defining elements listed as call-and-response, blues scale, specific chord progressions, blue notes (flattened 3rd/5th/7th); "few characteristics common to all blues". — [Wikipedia, Blues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blues) (secondary/popular)
- 12-bar form: three closely related chords (I, IV, V) in fixed order, commonly dominant 7ths, often ending in a turnaround; became increasingly common in the 1920s-30s with recorded singers. — [Wikipedia, Blues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blues)
- Early blues lacked a standard structure: Howard W. Odum documented secular songs c.1908-10 with freely repeated/extended verses; David Evans notes standardization came through commercial recording; pre-1920 field recordings (Lead Belly, Henry Thomas) show many structures other than 8/12/16 bars; Walter Vinson's "Sitting on Top of the World" is 9 bars. — [Wikipedia, Blues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blues) (relaying Evans/Odum; check originals)
- Lyric AAB: single line repeated four times in early verses; AAB became most common in first decades of 20th c.; W.C. Handy said AAB avoided the "monotony of lines repeated three times"; early published examples "Dallas Blues" (Wand, 1912), "St. Louis Blues" (Handy, 1914). — [Wikipedia, Blues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blues)
- Scholarly baseline in MTO: blues "usually associated with strophic form", "a series of verses, with a 12-bar blues progression underlying an aab melodic and lyrical structure", citing Titon (1977, 142), Taft (1983, xiii), von Appen & Frei-Hauenschild (2015, 48-49); de Clercq (2012, 124) calls the 12-bar aab the "classic" approach, dominant 1925-1966 in Carter's corpus. — [Carter, "Beyond Strophic: Prolonged Refrains, Choruses, and Bridges in the Blues, 1923-1966," Music Theory Online 25.3 (2025)](https://www.mtosmt.org/issues/mto.25.31.1/mto.25.31.1.carter.php)
- Newman White (1929, 207): "the stanza, and not the song, is the only true unit" (blues stanza as unit of analysis). — Carter, MTO (same link)
- Open Music Theory (Hughes/Lavengood, Gotham et al.): dominant 7th is the defining feature of blues harmony; all chords in standard 12-bar are dominant 7ths; blues emphasises plagal over authentic cadence. — [search summary of Open Music Theory "Blues Harmony"](https://viva.pressbooks.pub/openmusictheory/chapter/blues-harmony/) (page itself returned 403; summary only)
- Titon: "downhome blues scale" derived from 1926-30 recordings, spans a tenth rather than an octave (Titon 1977, 155, as reported). Titon's study rests on 47 transcriptions (44 downhome + 3 vaudeville), mostly first stanza only. — [Hein, "Blues tonality"](https://www.ethanhein.com/wp/2014/blu); [review/search result on Titon](https://mitpressbookstore.mit.edu/book/9780807844823)
- Van der Merwe (1992, 119, 123): most altered degrees are 3rd, 7th, 5th, 6th; thirds in performance are mostly neutral rather than major/minor; blue notes have "melodic dissonance". — Hein, "Blues tonality" (secondary report of Origins of the Popular Style)
- Everett (2004, 17): blues melodies decorate major-mode harmony; blues tritones/half steps "intrinsically dissonant". — Hein, "Blues tonality" (secondary)

### Inferences
- A rigorous "is it a blues" test must separate prototype (12-bar AAB I-IV-V7) from criteria actually necessary; scholars (Evans via Wikipedia, Odum) show the prototype is historically a later standardization.

- Could not access Grove "Blues" (Evans/Oliver), New Grove Dictionary of Jazz (Kernfeld), Schuller, Baraka, Murray's text itself, or Evans's *Big Road Blues*; no direct quotation obtained.

## 2. 8-bar and 16-bar blues: are they "blues"? How are 12+4 forms classified?

### Takeaway
Reference and instructional sources uniformly treat 8-bar and 16-bar forms as blues forms (variants of the 12-bar, or of the 8-bar), and list "Watermelon Man" as a 16-bar blues. But these sources are mostly popular/pedagogical and tertiary; the one peer-reviewed treatment found (Carter, MTO 2025) frames 16-bar as a verse-chorus (8+8) hybrid and argues the blues is "inherently hybrid", so non-12-bar forms remain blues.

### Cited Findings
- 8-bar blues: "second most common blues form" (Riker, *Complete Blues Guitar Method*, 1994, p. 91); IV typically at bar 3 not 5; I-V in bar 2 a characteristic (Riker); "common to folk, rock, and jazz forms" (Barrett 2000, p. 8). Examples: "Trouble in Mind" (Hill, 1926), "How Long Blues" (Carr, 1928), "Nobody Knows You When You're Down and Out" (B. Smith, 1929), "Key to the Highway" (Broonzy, 1941), "Worried Life Blues" (Big Maceo, 1941; "probably the most common eight-bar progression"). — [Wikipedia, Eight-bar blues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eight-bar_blues)
- 16-bar blues: "a variation on the standard twelve-bar blues, or less commonly on the eight-bar blues"; most versions lengthen one section or repeat a middle section/transition; also common in ragtime (Horn 1994, *Popular Music* 13/3, 366-369, review). Examples: "Hoochie Coochie Man" (extends opening tonic section), "Going Down the Road Feeling Bad" (repeats middle section), "Watermelon Man" (repeats the transition between chord 9 [V] and 10 [IV] twice -- claim flagged "citation needed"). — [Wikipedia, Sixteen-bar blues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sixteen-bar_blues)
- Wikipedia "Blues" lists "Watermelon Man" and "Sweet 16 Bars" (Ray Charles) with 8/16-bar progressions. — [Wikipedia, Blues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blues)
- "Varying the section lengths can produce eight-bar or sixteen-bar blues" (unsourced). — [Wikipedia, Twelve-bar blues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twelve-bar_blues)
- Music-theory-for-dummies style sources also list 24- and 32-bar blues. — [Dummies, popular genres and forms](https://www.dummies.com/article/academics-the-arts/music/music-theory/music-theory-popular-genres-and-forms-265008/) (search snippet only)
- Scholarly classification of 16 bars: de Clercq (2012, 142) calls an 8+8 form "hybrid 16-bar blues"; Carter reads it as verse (8) + chorus (8), clearer verse-chorus form; also 4+8 "prolonged refrain" (Titon 1977a, 326 n.46, "quatrain-refrain"), a "chorusification" of the 12-bar scheme. — [Carter, MTO 2025](https://www.mtosmt.org/issues/mto.25.31.1/mto.25.31.1.carter.php)
- Carter's inclusion criterion is genre-based: songs "typically categorized as a 'blues' song"; core claim: "Blues that connected with the popular mainstream formally and otherwise did not cease to be blues"; "the blues is an inherently hybrid genre at its heart" (citing Wald 2004, xiii-xiv, 232; Floyd 1995, 78); critiques Middleton (1972, 52-53) as "enforcing a notion of blues purity". — Carter, MTO 2025
- Von Appen & Frei-Hauenschild (2015, 22-23) define a harmonic "scheme" (slow harmonic rhythm, conventional pattern) -- a way to treat blues as a scheme rather than a fixed bar count. — Carter, MTO 2025

### Inferences
- Two classification logics: (a) "variant" (extension of 12-bar by lengthening/repeating a section) -- puts Watermelon Man inside blues; (b) "hybrid/verse-chorus" -- depends on whether sections are derived from blues phrase structure. Watermelon Man's 8+8 vs 12+4 reading matters (see section 5).
- Support for 8-/16-bar status is mostly pedagogical/encyclopedic; a report should flag that evidence quality.

### Gaps
- No Grove/Evans/Oliver/Titon statement on 8- or 16-bar forms found. No peer-reviewed source naming Watermelon Man found. "Trouble in Mind" 1926 recording attribution disagrees across sources (Bertha "Chippie" Hill vs Bertha Hill) -- unresolved.

## 3. Jazz terminology: "a blues," "blues-based," "bluesy," "minor blues"

### Takeaway
Little reliable material was accessible. Jazz-theory texts accessible via secondary sources treat "blues" as a named chord-change form with jazz variants (bebop blues, minor blues); I found no source defining "blues-based" vs "a blues."

### Cited Findings
- "Blues changes" = twelve-bar progression; jazz variant "bebop blues" resembles Parker's "Now's the Time," "Billie's Bounce," Rollins's "Tenor Madness" (Spitzer 2001, *Jazz Theory Handbook*, p. 62); minor 12-bar blues examples: Coltrane's "Equinox," "Mr. P.C." (Spitzer 2001, p. 63); V chord may be V7 or v7. — [Wikipedia, Twelve-bar blues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twelve-bar_blues)
- Quick-change ("quick to four") variant, IV in bar 2 (McCumber 2006, p. 25); V-IV-I-I in third line considered standard in shuffle blues (Gerow & Tanner 1984, p. 37). — same
- Albert Murray separates "blues" as mood from "blues music" (essentially dance music): "The blues as such are synonymous with low spirits. Blues music is not."; blues idiom = stylized music of creative artists. — [Washington Examiner](https://www.washingtonexaminer.com/magazine/799109/upbeat-downbeat/); [Pistelli on The Hero and the Blues](https://johnpistelli.com/2018/05/19/albert-murray-the-hero-and-the-blues/) (secondary)
- Wikipedia: "blues" as a distinct genre arose with the 1920s recording industry and Great Migration, as a marketing term; musicians often called themselves "songsters". — [Wikipedia, Blues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blues)

### Inferences
- "Blues" can denote (1) form/changes, (2) idiom (Murray), (3) marketing genre -- these yield different answers for Watermelon Man.

### Gaps
- No access to Levine, Coker, Aebersold, Berklee, Kernfeld, Real Book genre labels. Web search for Levine returned only spam PDF sites (discarded). "Blues-based/bluesy" distinctions: nothing found.

## 4. Blues tonality: tonality, mode, form, or style?

### Takeaway
Scholars generally treat blues tonality as a scale/pitch-set phenomenon (blue notes, neutral intervals) layered over, or in tension with, major-mode I-IV-V harmony; several argue for a distinct system. No consensus; dominant-7th tonic as a stable tonic is widely noted.

### Cited Findings
- Kubik: blues tonality grounded in lower/middle overtones of scales degrees 1 and 4 (Kubik 2008, 176-177); blue notes "in tune" relative to original pitch set; V7 "frequently absent, especially in rural blues" (Kubik 2005, 207). — [Hein, "Blues tonality"](https://www.ethanhein.com/wp/2014/blues-tonality/) (secondary relay; verify against Kubik, *Africa and the Blues* 1999 / 2005-2008 works)
- Fahey (on Charley Patton): two "blues modes" -- Dorian+Mixolydian union, and Mixolydian alone. — Hein
- Tagg: blues scale as extension of minor pentatonic; blues tonality substitutes a major triad for the tonic of diatonic minor/Dorian; loop grooves create "places to be" rather than large-scale tonal scheme. — Hein (2009; no page)
- Weisethaunet (2001, 101): blue notes as expressive pitch play inseparable from rhythm and timbre. Tallmadge (1984, 155): pitches as "mobile, unstable units". Cutting (2018): vocal-pitch analysis, three blue-note clusters (neutral third, between 4 and #4, around b7); possible derivation from just 5:6:7:9. Jaffe (2011, 35-37) defends "major blues scale" and bVI7. Tymoczko (2011, 374) reads blues as deliberate harmonic clash (disputed by Hein). — Hein
- Dominant-7th chords in blues "default sound rather than a force pulling toward resolution" (interpretive). — [Beatkitchen](https://beatkitchen.io/guides/musician-basics/12-blues-and-the-stuff-we-didnt-cover/) (low-grade, popular)

### Inferences
- The "dominant-seventh tonic is stable" idea and the Tagg/Fahey modal accounts mean I7-IV7-V7 harmony can itself signal blues tonality independent of bar count.

- Not obtained: Gauldin, Doll *Hearing Harmony*, Moore, Everett primary texts; Van der Merwe and Kubik primary pages (reported only via Hein, a blog).

## 5. Watermelon Man: descriptions found

### Cited Findings
- "essentially a sixteen-bar blues: the first 8 bars mimic a classic blues exactly, whilst the following 8 are an elongated variation of the regular final section"; in F, straight-eighths groove; "a healthy dose of the blues with a bit of gospel". — [JazzFuel, Watermelon Man](https://jazzfuel.com/watermelon-man/) (popular)
- Chart (F): F7 x4 | Bb7 x2, F7 x2 | C7 Bb7 C7 Bb7 | C7 Bb7 F7 x2 (i.e. I7 4 bars, IV7 2, I7 2, then V7/IV7 alternation 4, then V7 IV7 I7 I7). — [saxstation](https://saxstation.com/watermelon-man-by-herbie-hancock.htm) (teaching site; one source; verify against the published lead sheet)
- Wikipedia lists it under 16-bar blues (repeat of the V-IV transition), flagged "citation needed". — [Wikipedia, Sixteen-bar blues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sixteen-bar_blues)

### Inferences
- On the saxstation chart, bars 1-8 are I-I-I-I-IV-IV-I-I (a blues first 8 with the 12-bar's bars 1-8 in standard order), bars 9-16 are V-IV-V-IV-V-IV-I-I = the 12-bar's V-IV-I-I tail with the V-IV doubled. So it is read as 12-bar + repeated V-IV (a "variant" account) -- but a 4/4/8 or 8/8 (verse/refrain) view is also possible; sources disagree with each other on which framing.
- Under criteria from sections 1-4: dominant-7th I-IV-V ✔; extended 12-bar phrase logic ✔; AAB lyric N/A (instrumental; melody is riff-based -- not checked); blue-note melody not verified here.
