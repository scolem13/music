# Watermelon Man (Hancock, 1962): form, harmony, origins, later versions

Method note: about 15 web calls. I did not reach a Real Book / Hal Leonard chart, jazzstandards.com, iReal Pro, the Hancock memoir text, Pond's book, or the NPR transcript (the NPR fetch timed out twice; jazzguitar.be returned 403). Hancock quotes below are taken from secondary pages that quote him, not from the primary text. I could not check them against the memoir.

## 1. Exact form of the 1962 head and chord changes

### Takeaway
The head is 16 bars in F. It is a 12-bar-blues skeleton with the last four-plus bars reworked: bars 1-8 are a standard blues opening, and the bar 9-10 pair (C7 / Bb7) is stated three times before a 2-bar return to F7. Every source with chords uses dominant sevenths on F, Bb and C only; none shows an Eb7 or other bVII chord in the 1962 head.

### Cited Findings
- Bar-by-bar chart (one community forum post, not an authoritative lead sheet): bars 1-4 F7; 5-6 Bb7; 7-8 F7; 9 C7; 10 Bb7; 11 C7; 12 Bb7; 13 C7; 14 Bb7; 15-16 F7. The key is not stated on the page. The page itself calls the form "a blues modified a little bit". — [saxstation.com](https://saxstation.com/watermelon-man-by-herbie-hancock.htm)
- Ethan Hein's analysis agrees with that chart: "It's essentially a twelve-bar blues in F, but with bars nine and ten repeated three times." He adds that the last two bars are harmonically identical to the first four, so chorus boundaries are hard to hear, and Hancock exploits this on the 1973 recording. (Counting: 8 + 3x2 + 2 = 16.) — [Ethan Hein](https://ethanhein.com/wp/2022/watermelon-man/)
- Key of F: "It is most commonly played in the key of F"; "This 16 bar blues is played in a straight eight groove, originally a latin-rock style, and later funk." — [learnjazzstandards.com](https://learnjazzstandards.com/?p=6627). Hooktheory also lists F major (128 BPM, 4/4) with I7, IV7 and V7 chords, though the page's chord-string notation is unclear. — [Hooktheory](https://www.hooktheory.com/theorytab/view/herbie-hancock/watermelon-man)
- JazzFuel says: 16-bar blues, "first 8 bars follow a classic blues pattern, and the last 8 are an extended variation of the final section"; written in F; no chord changes given. It points to a Hancock-chaired Jazz Day score (jazzday.com, "WATERMELON-MAN-s2-Full-Score.pdf") that I did not retrieve. — [JazzFuel](https://jazzfuel.com/watermelon-man/)
- Wikipedia: "sixteen-bar blues form", drawing on R&B, soul jazz and bebop; Hancock's chordal work draws on gospel. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Watermelon_Man_(composition))
- The Herbie Hancock official site's Takin' Off page quotes the liner notes as "a strongly gospel-infused 16-bar theme". It says the opening piano vamp is a gospel-style figure (quarter rest, two eighths, an eighth and a dotted quarter), the horns state the theme mainly in unison, solos run Hubbard, Gordon, Hancock, and the master runs 7:09. It says Lion immediately heard the potential in the "modified blues". — [herbiehancock.com](https://www.herbiehancock.com/music/discography/album/takin-off/)
- A UVM blog says dominant sevenths on "the four chords of the original" (contrast Cantaloupe Island's minor sevenths) and a 16-bar form with a similar bassline and piano figure to Cantaloupe Island. I count three chords in the saxstation chart, so "four" disagrees with that chart. — [UVM blog](https://blog.uvm.edu/tgcleary/2020/03/10/from-watermelon-man-to-cantaloupe-island-and-beyond-herbie-hancocks-ingenious-reinventions-featuring-caves-of-the-island-an-original-tune-based-on-cantaloupe-island/)
- Personnel: Hancock, Dexter Gordon, Freddie Hubbard, Butch Warren, Billy Higgins. Recorded May 1962 (uDiscover). — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Watermelon_Man_(composition)); [uDiscover](https://www.udiscovermusic.com/stories/watermelon-man-herbie-hancock-feature)
- Billy Higgins' feel, per Hancock (via uDiscover, quoting Possibilities): "somewhere between straight eighth notes and the swinging triplets of jazz" and a "great funky-jazz flavor". — [uDiscover](https://www.udiscovermusic.com/stories/watermelon-man-herbie-hancock-feature)

### Inferences
- The C7 (V) to Bb7 (IV) alternation in bars 9-14 is a V-IV oscillation, which is a normal blues turnaround element, not a bVII-IV cadence. The suggested Eb7 does not appear in any source I found for the 1962 head; the only flat-side chords found are in the 1973 version (section 3).
- This is a 12-bar blues with bars 9-12 (V IV V IV) stretched by one extra V-IV pair and a 2-bar tonic tag, which matches "sixteen-bar blues" usage.

### Gaps
- No published lead sheet checked (Real Book, Hancock songbook, iReal, jazzstandards.com). Whether published charts differ from the record (e.g. substituted chords, 9ths, or a different key) is unverified.
- No source I found describes the melody note by note (blue notes, minor pentatonic, blues scale). Only the general descriptions above exist. The melody having blue thirds or a minor pentatonic basis is unverified here; an ABC transcription from the Jazz Day score would settle it.
- No source describes the bass line in detail beyond "similar bassline and piano accompaniment figure" to Cantaloupe Island, and the gospel-style piano vamp rhythm above. Whether the vamp is in F with b7 chord tones is not stated.
- No source found for whether F is the written key in the Real Book.

## 2. Hancock's own account

### Takeaway
Hancock ties the tune to the cry and horse-drawn wagon of a Chicago watermelon seller, says it took about 15 minutes to write, and says he wanted something real rather than a purely commercial tune. In the sources I reached he does not call it "a blues" or "bluesy"; that word comes from secondary writers.

### Cited Findings
- 1962 Takin' Off liner notes (Hancock): "In reflecting on my childhood I recalled the cry of the watermelon man making his rounds through the back streets and alleys" (truncated in the source). Leonard Feather's notes mention the 1938 Ellington record of the same title: "No connection." — [herbiehancock.com](https://www.herbiehancock.com/music/discography/album/takin-off/). Another page gives the same line with "Chicago's South Side" wording, which is a secondary quotation. — [Wikipedia, citing Santoro 2004 p. 86](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Watermelon_Man_(composition))
- Possibilities (2014), as quoted by uDiscover: "I grew up hearing the clackety-clack, clackety-clack of his horse-drawn wagon." / "I'd heard the rhythmic clacking so many times, it was easy to turn it into a song patter." / "I wrote out a funky arrangement, with the melody lilting over a rhythmic pattern". Also "The only moment I remember worrying about was just before we played 'Watermelon Man.'" — [uDiscover](https://www.udiscovermusic.com/stories/watermelon-man-herbie-hancock-feature). Not verified against the book.
- MOJO interview: "the thing that kept hitting me was the watermelon man"; the vendor went "through the cobblestone alleys of Chicago with a horse-drawn carriage on wagonwheels, not tyres"; "It took 15 minutes to write."; "I don't wanna write a, quote, commercial tune just for the heck of it. I want it to be real."; Donald Byrd's advice: "half the record is for yourself, and half the record is for the company." On the blues generally: "I'm from Chicago so I heard the real blues" and "I heard the real blues, Muddy Waters and Howlin' Wolf and a bunch of others." — [MOJO](https://www.mojo4music.com/articles/stories/herbie-hancock-interviewed/)
- Wikipedia states Hancock wrote the tune to help sell his debut and it was the first piece he composed with a commercial goal; he did not regard it as a sellout and called it one of his strongest pieces structurally (Lyons, 1989). That is Wikipedia's paraphrase, not an exact Hancock quote. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Watermelon_Man_(composition))
- Ethan Hein says Hancock got the idea by transcribing street calls from women in his neighbourhood, citing context from a 1985 Steven Feld interview (a different quote from that interview about Ba-Benzele: "I could do it but I know that it's OK 'cause its just a brothers kind of thing."). — [Ethan Hein](https://ethanhein.com/wp/2022/watermelon-man/)
- Liner notes via the official site: Hancock played the tune for Alfred Lion at his Blue Note audition and Lion asked for more originals. Donald Byrd urged him to play it for Mongo and to form Hancock Music publishing. — [herbiehancock.com](https://www.herbiehancock.com/music/discography/album/takin-off/)

### Inferences
- The only "bluesy/soul-jazz" characterisation I found comes from reviewers and the label-site notes ("modified blues", "gospel-infused"). Hancock's own words in the reached sources stress street-vendor calls, Chicago, gospel/R&B listening and a commercial but "real" intent. The report should not attribute "blues" to Hancock.

### Gaps
- Did not read the NPR 2014 transcript (https://www.npr.org/transcripts/354871509 surfaced in search but the fetch timed out), JazzTimes, DownBeat, Guardian, or the memoir text. The Lyons 1989 and Santoro 2004 citations are only second-hand through Wikipedia. Exact memoir wording about the "women calling out" or any "bluesy" intention is not located. One search summary said the mother was involved; no source confirmed this.

## 3. Mongo Santamaria 1963

### Takeaway
Mongo's hit keeps the same 16-bar form and F7/Bb7/C7 harmony, slower, with a Latin (guajira-based) groove and congas, and one analyst hears two extra groove bars on the second head. It reached No. 10 on the Hot 100 (some sources say 11).

### Cited Findings
- Hancock briefly subbed for Chick Corea in Mongo's band; Donald Byrd urged him to play the tune; Mongo recorded a three-minute radio version on 17 Dec 1962, re-recorded for Battle; released early 1963 (infobox Feb 1963); No. 10 Hot 100 in April 1963; Grammy Hall of Fame 1998; sometimes called the start of boogaloo. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Watermelon_Man_(composition)). JazzTimes also says No. 10. — [JazzTimes obituary](https://www.jazztimes.com/?p=1178). Conflict: uDiscover says "No. 11". — [uDiscover](https://www.udiscovermusic.com/stories/watermelon-man-herbie-hancock-feature)
- Form per Ethan Hein: the same as Hancock's except that "on the second pass through the head Mongo adds two bars of groove"; slower, guajira-based Latin groove, melody dragged behind the beat. — [Ethan Hein](https://ethanhein.com/wp/2022/watermelon-man/)
- Hancock (1998 notes, via official site): Mongo said "Keep playing!" and described it as "a tune the Cubans sang up in the mountains, a folk tune." — [herbiehancock.com](https://www.herbiehancock.com/music/discography/album/takin-off/)

### Gaps
- No chord chart for Mongo's version; the claim that harmony is unchanged rests on Hein's "form matches" statement only. Published sources on the Battle record's tempo, key and arrangement credits were not reached.

## 4. Head Hunters (1973)

### Takeaway
The 1973 track is a re-arrangement in funk: about 75 BPM, no solos, an added chord and a new section modulating up a minor third replace the final bars. Sources agree on the Hindewhu intro; the details of the added chords vary.

### Cited Findings
- Reworked by Hancock and Harvey Mason; only Head Hunters track not written for the album; 6:29. Personnel: Hancock (Rhodes, clavinet, synths), Maupin (soprano), Summers (percussion, beer bottle), Mason, Paul Jackson. — [Wikipedia: Head Hunters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Head_Hunters); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Watermelon_Man_(composition))
- Wikipedia: Hancock "added an eight-bar section"; Summers blows into bottles for the intro and outro imitating hindewhu; inspiration was the 1966 recording by Simha Arom and Geneviève Taurelle (the Ba-Benzele Pygmies). Wikipedia's Head Hunters article marks the beer-bottle credit "[citation needed]". — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Watermelon_Man_(composition))
- Hein's section map: about 75 BPM; no solos; "The last two bars of the form are replaced by a new section whose key jumps up a minor third"; sections labelled A (first half of 1962 head), B (bars 9-12 of the 1962 version) and C (new). Sequence: 15-bar hindewhu intro, bass enters (4), drums enter (4), clavinet (8), B (6), C (9), A (7, first Eb held over from the final Gb7 of C), B (6), C (3), A (7), B (6), C (9), outro groove (7), hindewhu fade. — [Ethan Hein](https://ethanhein.com/wp/2022/watermelon-man/)
- UVM blog: the 1973 version kept "its progression (expanded to include one more chord, Ab7) and a vestige of its melody" and added a new groove and electric instruments. — [UVM blog](https://blog.uvm.edu/tgcleary/2020/03/10/from-watermelon-man-to-cantaloupe-island-and-beyond-herbie-hancocks-ingenious-reinventions-featuring-caves-of-the-island-an-original-tune-based-on-cantaloupe-island/)
- Hein notes Madonna's "Sanctuary" sample is a Mixolydian groove clashing with a natural-minor melody on top. — [Ethan Hein](https://ethanhein.com/wp/2022/watermelon-man/)

### Inferences
- Hein's "Gb7" and UVM's "Ab7" are two different flat-side chords (a minor third above F7 would put chords in Ab). They may both be real (both are bVII-side/bIII-side colour); I cannot reconcile them without a transcription. Both are the only place any flat-side chord appears, and they belong to the 1973 version only.
- The 1973 head is a re-built form that is no longer a plain 16-bar blues; the groove (Mixolydian vamp) is still blues-derived.

### Gaps
- Pond's 2005 book, the Head Hunters liner notes, and a Hancock 1979 Down Beat quote ("the attention is on the rhythmic interplay...", via Wikipedia) were not read. No reliable chart of the 1973 harmony found. Bar counts in Hein's map do not obviously sum to a single-form repeat; treat as one listener's analysis.
