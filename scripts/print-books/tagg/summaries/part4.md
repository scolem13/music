## Chapter 12: Chord shuttles (pp. 371–400)

A chord shuttle is a pattern that swings back and forth between just two chords, like a seesaw. Tagg says it works as a shuttle when the whole to-and-fro fits into the "extended present", the few seconds our brain hears as one "now" (p. 371). The two chords get about equal time, and each can be heard as a complete chord in its own right. Because of this, a shuttle does not need a "home" chord. It does not have to lead anywhere.

That is the chapter's big idea. Many pop songs do not use harmony as a journey from tension to relief. Instead the shuttle is a "place to be", a mood you stay in (p. 379). Tagg sorts shuttles by the distance between the two chords (a step up, a fourth, a fifth, a sixth, a seventh) and lists real songs for each type, mostly English-language hits from 1955 to 2005. He also links types to feelings. The "aeolian" shuttle (a minor home chord and the major chord on the flat sixth note, for example Dm and B-flat) often goes with ominous, cold or waiting lyrics (pp. 386–387). Shuttles that go nowhere, like the one in the Human League song, help set a groove and a mood rather than tell a story. If you hear the same two chords for most of a song, you are hearing a state, not a process.

**Key ideas**
- A shuttle is two chords swapping back and forth, with roughly equal time on each, repeated at least twice, and short enough to feel like one "now" (p. 371).
- Shuttles need no "tonic" (home chord). The same two chords can be home-and-away in one song and the reverse in another (pp. 372–373, 399).
- Plagal shuttles (between a chord and the chord a fourth above it, such as D and G) are the most common type in pop and rock (pp. 375–376, 399).
- Some shuttles are a "place to be": a mood you stay in, not a path to somewhere else. Examples are Pink Floyd's Gm7 to C and the Human League's F to G (pp. 379, 392–394).
- Shuttles between a minor home chord and the major chord on the flat sixth (the aeolian shuttle) are often linked to words about fear, waiting, cold or sadness (pp. 386–387).
- Shuttles between the home chord and the chord a whole step below it (mixolydian shuttles) are linked to waiting and often appear in intros (pp. 389–391).
- Old folk tunes often do not fit a strict shuttle, so players use a "counterpoise sandwich" of tonic, other chord, tonic (pp. 396–397).

**Example pieces**
- Pink Floyd, *The Great Gig In The Sky* (Gm7 to C shuttle for over half the track) (pp. 378–380)
- Human League, *Don't You Want Me Baby* (F to G shuttle for 66% of the song) (pp. 391–392)
- Rolling Stones, *Satisfaction* (E to A, a plagal shuttle) (p. 373)
- The Champs, *Tequila* (F to E-flat) (p. 394)

**Try this**
Pick a song from the examples (try *Satisfaction* or *Tequila*). Listen once and decide: does it feel like it is going somewhere, or just staying in one spot? Then play the two chords back and forth on a keyboard or guitar for 30 seconds. Describe the mood in one word.

## Chapter 13: Chord loops 1 (pp. 401–420)

A chord loop is a short pattern of three or four chords that goes round and round, like a runner on a track. A shuttle goes back and forth; a loop goes in a circle (p. 402). Tagg chooses the word "loop" because musicians already know loops from recording software and old audio tape (p. 401). He separates loops from "turnarounds", short chord patterns at the end of a section that push the music back to the start (p. 402).

The chapter's main example is the "vamp": a four-chord pattern such as I–vi–ii–V (in C that is C, Am, Dm, G). It comes from jazz standards and old musicals, and each chord is a step "flatward" from the one before. In the late 1950s and early 1960s, American teen pop used a close cousin, I–vi–IV–V (C, Am, F, G), so much that Tagg calls it the sound of "milksap" music (pp. 407–408). Tagg then shows that 1950s rock'n'roll took a different path, built on the 12-bar blues and its V–IV–I ending, which is not the classical V–I move (pp. 412–414). He ends with the Beatles, who mixed many harmonic styles (pp. 417–418). Why it matters: the chords in a pop song tell you which tradition it comes from.

**Key ideas**
- A loop is a cycle of usually three or four chords, repeated at least once (p. 419).
- A loop usually lasts about 3 to 8 seconds. With a pause in the middle it can last up to about 18 seconds. Longer cycles, such as a 12-bar blues, are "cycles", not loops (p. 411).
- A turnaround and a loop can use the same chords. The difference is the job: a turnaround signals "the section is ending, go back to the start" (p. 411).
- The vamp, I–vi–ii–V, goes three steps "flatward" round the circle of fifths. The version I–vi–IV–V is nearly the same because ii and IV share almost the same notes (pp. 407, 411).
- 1950s rock'n'roll used the 12-bar blues and a V–IV–I ending, with no classical V–I cadence. Tagg says "dominant" and "subdominant" do not fit this music well (pp. 412–414).
- Loop chords can be called outgoing, medial (middle) or incoming, depending on where they sit in the loop (p. 414).
- The Beatles' music mixed vamps, blues, folk and other harmony styles (pp. 417–418).

**Example pieces**
- *Blue Moon* (Rodgers, 1934), with I–vi–ii–V used as loops and turnarounds (p. 405)
- Ketty Lester, *Love Letters* (1962), a vamp too slow to feel like a short loop (pp. 409–410)
- Eddie Cochran, *C'mon Everybody* (1958), a loop of I, IV and V with no classical dominant move (pp. 413–414)
- Bobby Darin, *Dream Lover* and Ben E. King, *Stand By Me*, from the I–vi–IV–V list (p. 409)

**Try this**
Play C, Am, F, G on a keyboard or guitar, one chord per bar, and repeat. Then sing a "doo-wop" tune on top. Next play C, Am, Dm, G and notice how little changes.

## Chapter 14: Chord loops & bimodality (pp. 421–450)

The same three chords can feel different depending on which one you hear as "home". D–C–G can be ionian (a normal major scale) if G is home, or mixolydian (a major scale with a lowered 7th) if D is home (p. 426). So how do we know which? Tagg's best clue is the anacrusis, the lead-in or pickup that rushes towards one chord. The chord that gets the clearest lead-in is probably home (p. 429). La Bamba and Sweet Home Alabama use the same chords but sound different for this reason (p. 430). Ionian loops head "flatward" round the circle of fifths; mixolydian loops head "sharpward" (clockwise) (pp. 431–432).

The chapter also introduces "bimodal" music: tunes that can be heard in two modes, with two possible home chords. The Argentine scholar Carlos Vega said there are no major tunes and minor tunes, just bimodal ones (p. 436). Reversing a progression can swap one mode for another (p. 441). Last, Tagg looks at loops built on the third chord (mediant), such as the "rock dorian" loop in Green Onions (p. 442). This matters because it explains why so much rock sounds "not quite major or minor".

**Key ideas**
- The four main ionian loops are I–IV–V–V (the La Bamba loop), I–V–IV–V, I–IV–V–IV and I–V–IV–IV (p. 448).
- The same chords can be ionian or mixolydian. The lead-in (anacrusis) is the best clue to which chord is home (pp. 426–430, 448).
- Mixolydian loops move sharpward in rising fifths, for example bVII–IV–I (pp. 431, 449).
- Aeolian loops also move sharpward, but two steps at a time (p. 433).
- Bimodal means a tune or cadence that sits between two modes, so one chord can be home or not (pp. 436, 449–450).
- Reversing the order of a progression can turn it into another mode, a trick called bimodal reversibility (pp. 440–441, 449).
- A "double shuttle" is a loop made of two shuttles, for example Otis Redding's G–B–C–A (p. 445).

**Example pieces**
- *La Bamba* (Ritchie Valens, 1958) and *Sweet Home Alabama* (Lynyrd Skynyrd, 1974) (pp. 422, 427–430)
- *Hey Jude* (Beatles, 1968), mixolydian loop at the end (p. 431)
- Carlos Puebla, *¡Hasta siempre!* (aeolian to phrygian) (pp. 437–439)
- Booker T. and the MGs, *Green Onions* (1962), a rock dorian loop (p. 442)

**Try this**
Play D, C, G, G as a loop. First stress the G chord (start the pattern on G with a strong lead-in). Then stress D instead (put the lead-in just before D). Does the same loop change mood?

## Chapter 15: The Yes We Can chords (pp. 451–478)

This chapter applies everything so far to one real case: the 2008 Obama campaign video *Yes We Can*. Its music is a four-chord loop, G–B–Em–C (I–III–vi–IV), one chord per bar at 100 beats a minute (p. 452). Tagg asks what the chords mean and where they come from. His tool is IOCM, "interobjective comparison material", meaning other music that sounds like the piece you are studying (glossary).

He looks at many songs and rejects most of them. Some matched the chords but had a different speed, sound or what came next. He keeps a few: *A World Without Love*, *Creep* (Radiohead), *Let It Be*, *No Woman No Cry*, *All Together Now* and *Grita* (pp. 477–478). Tagg argues that several things combine. The easy first-position guitar chords sound folk-like and open to everyone, like songs by Woody Guthrie and Bob Dylan (pp. 456–458). The jump from I to III suggests "going somewhere else" (p. 469). The I–x–vi–IV pattern in other songs goes with words about moving from hardship to hope (Table 40, p. 474). The lesson: one chord sequence tells you little on its own. Meaning comes from the chords, speed, instruments and words together (p. 478).

**Key ideas**
- The loop is G–B–Em–C. Each chord gets one bar, and every chord is played with a simple strum on an acoustic guitar (pp. 452, 477).
- Other songs may share the chords but still not sound like it, because of tempo, instruments or continuation (pp. 460–466).
- The first chord, an open G on acoustic guitar, is the same sound that starts several Dylan and Guthrie songs (pp. 456–457).
- The move from I to III has a "going somewhere else" feeling (p. 469).
- The closing move from C to G (IV to I) is plagal, a gospel-like "Amen" move (pp. 455–456).
- Songs with the I–x–vi–IV pattern often put trouble and hope side by side in the lyrics (pp. 473–474).
- Harmony is only one part of meaning: tempo, instruments, voice and words count too (p. 478).

**Example pieces**
- *Sitting On The Dock Of The Bay* (Otis Redding, 1968), same I–III start but works like a double shuttle (pp. 462–463)
- *Creep* (Radiohead, 1992), I–III–IV–iv loop (pp. 467–468)
- *Let It Be* (Beatles, 1970) and *No Woman No Cry* (Bob Marley, 1974), I–V–vi–IV (p. 472)
- *Grita* (Jarabe de Palo, 1996), the Spanish-language song closest to the original (p. 459)

**Try this**
Play G, B, Em, C on guitar or keyboard, one chord per bar, slowly. Then play G, D, Em, C. Write two words for how each feels, then listen to *Let It Be* and compare.

## Glossary terms

**chord shuttle** — A pattern that swings back and forth between two chords, with about equal time on each. It is short enough to be heard as one "now". (Tagg p. 371)

**extended present** — A stretch of time (a few seconds) that we hear as one unit, "now". Tagg says a shuttle or a simple loop fits inside it. (Tagg p. 371)

**place to be** — Tagg's name for a shuttle or loop that is a lasting mood, not a path leading somewhere. (Tagg p. 379)

**unidirectional** — Going one way only. A "unidirectional" progression, like ii–V–I, heads in one direction towards a goal, unlike a shuttle that swings back and forth. (Tagg p. 377)

**plagal shuttle** — A shuttle between a chord and the chord a fourth above it, such as D and G. It is very common in pop and rock. (Tagg pp. 375, 399)

**aeolian shuttle** — A shuttle between a minor home chord and the major chord on the flat sixth note. It is often linked to ominous, cold or waiting moods. (Tagg p. 386)

**mixolydian shuttle** — A shuttle between the home chord and the major chord a whole step below it (I and bVII). It is common in intros and linked to waiting. (Tagg pp. 389, 391)

**chord loop** — A cycle of usually three or four chords that goes round and round, repeated at least once. (Tagg pp. 401, 419)

**turnaround** — A short chord sequence at the end of a section that leads back to the start. Some turnarounds can also be used as loops. (Tagg pp. 402, 411)

**vamp** — A chord sequence of the type I–vi–ii–V (or I–vi–IV–V), with the roots falling by fifths. It comes from jazz standards and musicals. (Tagg pp. 404, 419)

**milksap** — Tagg's word for the bland teen pop of about 1957 to 1963, built on the I–vi–IV–V vamp. (Tagg pp. 407, 411)

**anacrusis** — A short lead-in or pickup that pushes the music into the next chord. It helps show which chord is home. (Tagg pp. 429, 448)

**bimodal** — Music that can be heard in two modes, so that two chords can each sound like the tonic. (Tagg pp. 436, 449–450)

**IOCM (interobjective comparison material)** — Other music that sounds like or is built like the piece you are studying. It helps you work out what the piece means. (Tagg p. 452)
