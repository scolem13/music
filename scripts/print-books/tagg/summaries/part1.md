## Preface (pp. 11–44)

Philip Tagg wrote this book because the music theory taught in most schools was built for one kind of music: European classical music from about 1730 to 1910. That theory works well for Mozart. It works badly for the music most people hear every day. Think of a rock riff, a film theme or a game soundtrack. Tagg says that using words like "dominant" and "perfect cadence" on a song like La Bamba is like using a theory of fire to explain electricity. So he builds a new set of terms. He also looks at things school theory ignores, such as short repeating chord patterns, sounds from other cultures, and the feel of a groove.

The preface also gives the book's basic vocabulary and its shorthand for notes and chords. It says who the book is for: anyone who can read basic staff notation. And it admits that "everyday" really means the music Tagg knows, mostly English-language music from the late twentieth century, plus music from other places he studied for this edition. Why it matters to you: if you listen to pop, rock, film or game music, the book gives you tools that fit that music instead of forcing it into classical rules.

**Key ideas**
- Standard theory textbooks cover mainly European classical music, so they often can't explain common pop and rock chord patterns (p. 11).
- A "note" is any single sound. A "tone" is a note with a pitch you can hear. "Tonality" is the system a culture uses to organise tones (p. 16).
- The book focuses on one chord, two chords (a "shuttle") and three or four chords (a "loop"), because these short patterns make up much of modern popular music (p. 21).
- Scale degrees get numbers with a small hat (like 1, 2, 3). A flat sign before a number means a note one semitone lower than the major-scale version (p. 32).
- Roman numerals show chords by their position in the scale: capitals for major chords, lower case for minor chords (p. 36).
- Tagg says the ionian mode (the "major scale") is only one mode among many. He calls it the default only because of history (p. 37).

**Example pieces**
- La Bamba, Guantanamera, Wild Thing, Twist & Shout: the same chord loop (p. 11)
- Sweet Home Alabama: a rock song his theory has to explain (p. 19)
- It's Not Unusual (Tom Jones): a tune that uses only six notes (p. 22)
- Don't Stand So Close To Me (The Police): a chord shuttle you can find by time on a recording (p. 29)

**Try this**
Pick one song you like. Listen for the chords repeating. Count how many different chords there are before it starts over. Is it one, two, three or four? Write the number down. You are finding out whether the song is built on a chord shuttle or a loop.

## Chapter 1: Note, pitch, tone (pp. 45–64)

This chapter sorts out three words that people use loosely. A **note** is one separate sound with a start and an end. A **pitch** is how high or low a sound is. It depends on how fast the sound vibrates, and we count that in hertz (Hz). A **tone** is a note whose pitch you can clearly hear. A flute note is a tone. A hi-hat or a snare drum hit is a note but not really a tone.

Tagg then fixes a muddle. Classical theory calls music "tonal" only if it is in one European style, and "atonal" if it has no home note. But atonal logically means "without tones". Twelve-tone music is full of tones. So he says "tonal" just means "made of tones". "Tonality" means any system a culture uses to organise tones. He invents "tonical" for music that has a home note, the tonic. "Modal" does not mean "outside the rules" either. The chapter ends with timbre, the quality that makes a flute sound different from a clarinet playing the same note. Timbre depends on how a sound starts, lasts and ends, and on its overtones.

**Key ideas**
- A note is a single sound; a tone is a note with a pitch you can hear (p. 51).
- Pitch comes from vibration speed (frequency). Standard concert pitch is 440 Hz (p. 47).
- "Atonal" is a bad word for music with no tonic. Say "non-tonical" instead (p. 53).
- The tonic is the main reference tone. Music that has one is "tonical" (p. 57).
- Timbre is shaped by an envelope: attack, decay, sustain and release (p. 58).
- Each tone also has overtones (harmonics) at whole-number multiples of its main frequency. The mix of overtones helps make each instrument sound different (p. 61).

**Example pieces**
- Sweet Home Alabama (intro), shown as a MIDI piano roll (p. 46)
- Psycho (the shower scene) and The Birds: Hitchcock films with Herrmann's music (p. 52)
- Gimme All Your Lovin' (ZZ Top): overtones heard in a distorted guitar (p. 62, footnote)

**Try this**
Play one note on two different instruments or sounds, for example a piano and a synth. Keep the pitch and the volume the same. Listen to how each note begins (attack) and how it fades (release). Describe each in one sentence, then say which part helped you tell them apart.

## Chapter 2: Tuning, octave, interval (pp. 65–84)

This chapter is about how pitches are fixed and organised. There are two kinds of tuning. "Extra-octave" tuning sets one standard pitch so everyone agrees. Concert pitch (A = 440 Hz) is the example. "Intra-octave" tuning decides where the notes inside an octave sit.

An octave is the interval where one note has exactly twice the frequency of another. Every music culture treats two notes an octave apart as "the same note" in a different register. An interval is the distance in pitch between two tones. We measure it in semitones or in cents (100 cents = 1 semitone).

The key problem is that "pure" intervals, built on simple ratios, don't add up neatly. Stacking pure thirds does not land exactly on an octave. Equal temperament fixes that by making all twelve semitones the same size. That suits a piano and music that changes key a lot. But it is a poor fit for blues, folk, and the music of many cultures. Those use drones, or notes that fall between piano keys. The chapter ends with how guitars, banjos and other string instruments are tuned.

**Key ideas**
- Concert pitch (A = 440 Hz) was set as an international standard so musicians and instruments can play together (p. 65).
- An octave has a frequency ratio of 2:1, and all known music traditions treat octave-apart notes as the same note (p. 68).
- Intervals are measured in semitones and cents (p. 67).
- Names like "dominant" and "leading note" suit classical music but mislead for most other music. The seventh degree a whole tone below the tonic is better called the "subtonic" (p. 74).
- Equal temperament splits the octave into 12 equal semitones, but pure tuning sounds clearer. G-sharp and A-flat are the same pitch on a piano but can differ in pure tuning (p. 74).
- Some traditions use steps that don't fit the 12-semitone grid, for example quarter-tone steps in Arab music and the pelog scale of Java (p. 76).

**Example pieces**
- Satisfaction (Rolling Stones, first riff note) and Dancing Queen (Abba): used to show octave numbering (p. 69)
- Sweet Home Alabama and Hey Jude (end): the chord loop I, flat seven, IV (p. 72)
- Creep (Radiohead): a chord loop that would suffer if played in pure tuning (p. 75, footnote)

**Try this**
Find a piano or keyboard app. Play a note, then the note 12 semitones (an octave) above it. Hum both. Does it feel like the same note in a higher place? Now try your own voice: sing a short tune, then sing it an octave higher or lower. Notice that it is still the same tune.

## Chapter 3: Heptatonic modes (pp. 85–149)

A mode is a short list of the notes a piece of music uses, put in order inside one octave. "Heptatonic" means seven notes. Tagg argues that the major scale (which he calls the **ionian** mode) is just one mode among many. School theory treats it as normal and calls everything else "modal". He thinks that is unfair.

He walks through the seven "church" modes: ionian, dorian, phrygian, lydian, mixolydian, aeolian and locrian. Each has its own pattern of whole-tone and semitone steps, which gives it its own flavour. He shows that a mode does not decide the mood by itself. Aeolian can be gloomy in Gloomy Sunday or cheerful in God Rest You Merry, Gentlemen. A poll of "saddest songs" found most were in major keys.

The second half looks at modes from the Arab world, Turkey, Greece, Spain and the Balkans. Many have a "flat two" or a big step of an augmented second. These are often used in film and metal to make something sound "foreign" or "dark". Tagg argues listeners should hear them as the home sound of those places, not just as exotic spice.

**Key ideas**
- A mode is a tonal vocabulary reduced to one of each note in an octave. A real tune uses its notes in a particular way, not like a scale run (p. 86).
- Classical theory treats ionian as the norm, so "minor" notes need a flat sign. Tagg calls bending other modes toward ionian "ionianisation" (p. 90).
- "Tonal versus modal" makes no sense. All modes are tonal (p. 93).
- Each church mode has a unique scale-degree profile. For example, only the dorian has a flat third, a major sixth and a flat seventh together (p. 96).
- The same mode can feel sad or happy. Speed, rhythm, timbre and culture all matter (p. 111).
- An Arab maqam is a mode plus rules for how to move through it. Its octave is split into two four-note groups called tetrachords (jins) (p. 118).
- "Phrygian dominant" and "lydian dominant" are misleading names. Those modes have no dominant (p. 129).

**Example pieces**
- God Save The Queen: used to show how a real tune becomes a mode (p. 87)
- Scarborough Fair (Simon & Garfunkel): dorian (p. 100)
- The Simpsons theme (Danny Elfman): lydian flavour (p. 102)
- Barber's Adagio for Strings: phrygian (p. 101)
- Nirvana's Smells Like Teen Spirit and Lithium: aeolian (p. 109)
- Lawrence of Arabia (Maurice Jarre): a Western idea of "Arab-sounding" (p. 120)
- Y Viva España (Sylvia Vrethammar): tourist "flamenco" that is really in A minor (p. 132)

**Try this**
On white keys only, play a drone (hold or repeat the note) on D, and make up short melodies that end on D. That is dorian. Now do the same on A (aeolian) and on C (ionian). For each, write one word for how it feels. Then listen to a song in your list and ask: does that feeling match what the song says? Tagg shows it often doesn't.

## Glossary terms

**note** — One single sound that starts and ends. It can be high or low, or even have no clear pitch, like a drum hit. (Tagg p. 45)

**pitch** — How high or low a sound is. It comes from how fast the sound vibrates, measured in hertz (Hz). (Tagg p. 47)

**tone** — A note with a pitch you can clearly hear. A hi-hat is a note but not a tone. (Tagg p. 51)

**tonal** — Made of tones, or having the qualities of a tone. Tagg uses it for all music with pitched notes, not just classical music. (Tagg p. 51)

**tonality** — The system a music culture uses to organise its tones. Every culture has its own, so there is more than one. (Tagg p. 56)

**tonic** — The home note or keynote that a piece or section is built around. Music with a tonic is "tonical". (Tagg p. 57)

**timbre** — The sound quality that lets you tell two instruments apart even when they play the same pitch at the same volume. It comes from the attack, sustain, release and mix of overtones. (Tagg p. 58)

**octave** — The interval between two notes where the top one vibrates twice as fast as the bottom. Cultures treat them as the same note in two registers. (Tagg p. 68)

**interval** — The distance in pitch between two tones. We measure it in semitones or in cents (100 cents = 1 semitone). (Tagg p. 67)

**equal temperament** — The tuning that splits the octave into 12 equal semitones. It is used on pianos and keyboards, but it slightly changes the "pure" intervals. (Tagg p. 74)

**scale degree** — A note's number in a mode, counted from the tonic as 1. A flat sign before the number means one semitone lower than in the ionian mode. (Tagg p. 32)

**mode** — A tonal vocabulary reduced to one occurrence of each note, put in order inside one octave. The major scale is one mode (the ionian). (Tagg p. 86)

**ionian** — Tagg's word for the major scale. He avoids calling it the default or "normal", because it is only one mode among many. (Tagg p. 90)

**ionianisation** — The classical habit of treating the ionian mode as the norm, and bending other modes (such as minor) to fit it, for example by raising the seventh note. (Tagg p. 90)

**counterpoise** — The main balancing note or notes that contrast with the tonic in a tune. (Tagg p. 88)

**maqam** — The Arab idea of a mode, with extra rules about which notes to stress and how a melody should move. The octave is thought of as two tetrachords. (Tagg p. 114)
