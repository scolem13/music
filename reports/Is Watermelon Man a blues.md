# Watermelon Man earns its "(Blues)" label

**Label it "F Major (Blues)".** By the site's own definition (dominant-seventh chords on I and IV) the 1962 head qualifies exactly: every source that gives chords shows only F7, Bb7 and C7 in a 12-bar skeleton stretched to 16 bars. Every web form description found calls it a "16-bar blues", or an "altered" or "extended" one, and none says it is not a blues. The evidence has three limits. Almost all of it is educational or encyclopedic, with no peer-reviewed or print authority confirmed. Critics classify the tune by genre as hard bop, soul jazz, funk or boogaloo, and Hancock never calls it a blues in any source reached. The melody's blues content (blue notes, pentatonic basis) was not checked at all. "Blues" is therefore the right tonality tag on harmonic grounds. The "16-bar" detail belongs in a separate form field, not in the tonality label. Gioia (p. 446), the Real Book and Grove could shift the confidence but are unlikely to reverse the recommendation. The 1963 Mongo Santamaría version keeps the same form and harmony. The 1973 Head Hunters version is a rebuilt form and should not carry the label automatically.

## The harmony and form of the 1962 head sit inside the blues

The 1962 head is 16 bars in F. The best bar-by-bar chart found (a teaching site, not a published lead sheet) reads F7 for bars 1-4, Bb7 for 5-6, F7 for 7-8, then C7 / Bb7 / C7 / Bb7 / C7 / Bb7 in bars 9-14, and F7 for 15-16 ([saxstation](https://saxstation.com/watermelon-man-by-herbie-hancock.htm)). Ethan Hein's independent analysis agrees: "essentially a twelve-bar blues in F, but with bars nine and ten repeated three times" ([Ethan Hein](https://ethanhein.com/wp/2022/watermelon-man/)). Set against the standard 12-bar plan (I for four bars, IV for two, I for two, then V, IV and I), the first eight bars match exactly and the V-IV pair is simply stated three times before the tonic tag. That is 8 + 3×2 + 2 = 16.

The harmony is purely dominant sevenths on I, IV and V. No source shows an Eb7 or other flat-side chord in the 1962 head. A UVM blog mentions "four chords" ([UVM blog](https://blog.uvm.edu/tgcleary/2020/03/10/from-watermelon-man-to-cantaloupe-island-and-beyond-herbie-hancocks-ingenious-reinventions-featuring-caves-of-the-island-an-original-tune-based-on-cantaloupe-island/)), but that conflicts with the three-chord chart. Hooktheory lists I7, IV7 and V7 in F major ([Hooktheory](https://www.hooktheory.com/theorytab/view/herbie-hancock/watermelon-man)). Dominant-seventh chords on the tonic are the standard open-source description of blues harmony, and blues is commonly said to favour plagal motion (IV to I) over authentic cadences ([Open Music Theory, via search summary](https://viva.pressbooks.pub/openmusictheory/chapter/blues-harmony/)). Watermelon Man fits both, and the V-IV alternation is a normal blues turnaround device, not a modal or functional departure.

Two readings of the form exist. The "variant" reading treats it as a 12-bar blues with the V-IV tail doubled, which keeps it inside the blues. Peer-reviewed work on 16-bar forms instead tends to describe an 8+8 hybrid with verse and chorus character, though it argues that "blues that connected with the popular mainstream formally and otherwise did not cease to be blues" and that the genre is "inherently hybrid" ([Carter, Music Theory Online 2025](https://www.mtosmt.org/issues/mto.25.31.1/mto.25.31.1.carter.php)). Either reading keeps the tune a blues. The "variant" reading fits this tune's actual chords more closely, because bars 1-8 are an unaltered 12-bar opening.

## Published classifications agree on "16-bar blues" but lean on thin sourcing

Web databases and lesson sites converge. AllSolos lists the form as "Blues - 16 bars", "built on an extended blues structure" ([AllSolos](https://www.allsolos.com/tune/77/watermelon-man)). Standard Repertoire calls it an "Altered blues" of 16 bars ([Standard Repertoire](https://standardrepertoire.com/watermelon-man.html)). Learn Jazz Standards says "This 16 bar blues is played in a straight eight groove" ([learnjazzstandards.com](https://learnjazzstandards.com/?p=6627)). JazzFuel says "essentially a sixteen-bar blues: the first 8 bars mimic a classic blues exactly" ([JazzFuel](https://jazzfuel.com/watermelon-man/)). FretDojo says "it's a 16 bar blues, rather than a 12 bar blues" ([FretDojo](https://fretdojo.com/watermelon-man-guitar/)). Wikipedia states the tune "is written in a sixteen-bar blues form" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Watermelon_Man_(composition))), and its Sixteen-bar blues page cites the tune as an example with no inline source. The three references on that page do not mention it ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sixteen-bar_blues)). The Herbie Hancock site quotes the 1962 liner notes calling it a "modified blues" and a "strongly gospel-infused 16-bar theme" ([herbiehancock.com](https://www.herbiehancock.com/music/discography/album/takin-off/)). Counting these as independent confirmations would overstate the evidence, since several sites likely echo one another.

The critical frame is different. Reviewers and encyclopedias use genre terms: hard bop and soul jazz for the album, and "bluesy" for the track ([Wikipedia, Takin' Off](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Takin%27_Off)). Others cite R&B, soul jazz and bebop influences, and call the Head Hunters version a "radical jazz-funk makeover" ([uDiscover](https://www.udiscovermusic.com/stories/watermelon-man-herbie-hancock-feature)). No dissenting source arguing "not a blues" was located, though the search for one was limited.

## Idiom and Hancock's own account pull toward "blues-derived," not "a blues"

Hancock describes the origin as the cry and horse-drawn wagon of a Chicago watermelon seller, says "It took 15 minutes to write", and says he wanted the tune to be "real" rather than merely commercial ([MOJO](https://www.mojo4music.com/articles/stories/herbie-hancock-interviewed/)). In the same interview he says he grew up in Chicago hearing "the real blues, Muddy Waters and Howlin' Wolf". In no source reached does he call this tune a blues. Billy Higgins' feel is described, via Hancock, as "somewhere between straight eighth notes and the swinging triplets of jazz" ([uDiscover](https://www.udiscovermusic.com/stories/watermelon-man-herbie-hancock-feature)). The straight-eighths groove, gospel piano vamp and funk orientation place it in the soul-jazz and boogaloo idiom more than in a traditional shuffle blues.

This matters because "blues" has three meanings that give different answers. As a form or chord scheme it is a blues. As a marketing genre (blues as a category of 1920s recording) it is not. As an idiom in Albert Murray's sense of stylised music of creative artists ([Pistelli on Murray](https://johnpistelli.com/2018/05/19/albert-murray-the-hero-and-the-blues/), secondary) it plausibly is. Scholars also caution that the 12-bar AAB I-IV-V prototype is a later standardisation, since early blues had many structures ([Wikipedia, Blues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blues), relaying Evans and Odum), so a strict 12-bar test would be historically arbitrary. The site's tag measures harmony, so the first meaning is the relevant one.

## Mongo 1963 matches; Head Hunters 1973 does not

Mongo Santamaría's hit keeps the form per Hein, with two extra groove bars on the second head, and a slower guajira-based groove. It reached No. 10 on the Hot 100 (uDiscover says No. 11) and entered the Grammy Hall of Fame in 1998 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Watermelon_Man_(composition)); [JazzTimes](https://www.jazztimes.com/?p=1178)). The claim that its harmony is unchanged rests only on Hein's statement that the form matches, with no chart found. The 1973 Head Hunters track is about 75 BPM, has no solos, adds a chord (Hein hears a Gb7 and a new section a minor third up; UVM says Ab7) and replaces the final bars ([Ethan Hein](https://ethanhein.com/wp/2022/watermelon-man/)). The two accounts of the added chords conflict and no transcription resolves them. A tag on the composition would still be "F Major (Blues)". A per-recording tonality tag for Head Hunters should not use "(Blues)" without a chart showing which chords sit where, since the new section leaves the I-IV-V frame.

## The three options for the site

"(Blues)" is the one the data supports. It matches the site's definition, uses no unverified claim, and lets a student find the tune alongside other dominant-seventh pieces. "(16-bar blues)" is accurate to the web sources but defeats the tag's purpose. It would create a new category for a single tune, misleads on 8-bar and 12-bar equivalence, and states a form fact inside a tonality field. If the site has a bar-count or form field, put "16 bars (12-bar blues with the V-IV pair repeated)" there. Not labelling would be defensible only if the site treated "Blues" as a genre judgement, which its own definition says it does not. The weakest honest objection is that "Blues" may suggest a shuffle feel and blue-note melody to a student. A short note on the tune page, that the groove is straight-eighths soul jazz, covers that.

## What could not be verified and what checking would change

Print authorities were not reachable: the Real Book and Sher charts, Gioia's *The Jazz Standards* (the entry is on p. 446 per a catalogue listing; its wording was not obtained), Grove and *New Grove Dictionary of Jazz*, the Penguin Guide and AllMusic review text, Levine, Coker, Aebersold and Pond's Head Hunters book. The chord chart rests on one teaching site plus Hein, not a published lead sheet. Key, chord extensions and any substitutions in the Real Book version are unchecked. The melody was not analysed: no source describes blue thirds, minor pentatonic or the blues scale, and an ABC transcription from the Jazz Day score would settle it. The Hancock quotes come from secondary pages, not the memoir or the NPR transcript. The Wikipedia entry for the tune cites Lyons 1989 and Santoro 2004 only second-hand.

Checking would change the following. A published chart with the same F7/Bb7/C7 changes would confirm the label with no edits. Gioia or a lead sheet calling it a "blues" would add a print authority for "16-bar blues" and could justify a form note quoting it. A chart with substituted chords (for example extended or altered dominants) would not alter the "dominant sevenths on I and IV" test unless the I or IV chord is not dominant. A melody showing no blues-scale content would argue for a footnote rather than removing the tag, because the site's definition is harmonic. Only a respected source stating the tune is not a blues, or a chart where I and IV are not dominant sevenths, would justify dropping the label. The searches found neither, though the search for dissent was limited.

## Conclusion

The question mixes form, harmony and genre, and the answer differs by lens: by harmony and form the 1962 head is a blues, by recording-industry genre it is soul jazz, and by Hancock's own account it is a street-cry tune. The site's tonality tag measures only the first, so "(Blues)" is correct and low-risk. The useful novel point is that the "16" only describes a stretched tail, with bars 1-8 identical to a standard blues. A form field can state this precisely without inventing a new tonality category, and the Head Hunters recording is the case that needs separate handling.
