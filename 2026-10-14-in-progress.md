## One shared ABC parser, toward a notation system with MIDI and audio entry (a separate session)

The goal, agreed in chat: one notation system built from the tune, tonal and rhythm assessments, Song Builder and
Chord/Melody Entry. You set the parameters first (key, meter, allowed rhythm cells, strictness), then enter by MIDI,
tapping or microphone, and a decoder ranks what you might have meant (an extended `MelodyDwim`). Microphone input is
built **last**, but the design allows for it from the start: every event carries a confidence, and noisy MIDI stands in
for the microphone in tests.

## Guitar

Conventions used in both sequences. Standard tuning. Fret numbers are written low E to high e, six characters, `x` = not played (muted or skipped), e.g. `x32010`; two-digit frets are separated by spaces where needed (`8 x 8 9 8 x`). Fingers: 1 = index, 2 = middle, 3 = ring, 4 = little, T = thumb. Strings are numbered 6 (low E) to 1 (high e), as in the site's fretboard diagrams. Terms follow the Chord Library: **CAGED shapes** (the open C, A, G, E, D shapes moved up the neck; search words `caged`, `Eshape`), **drop 2**, **drop 3**, **shell (R-3-7)**, **three-string triads**, **7ths, no 5th**, **guide tones (3rd and 7th)**, **string set** (`set:4321`), **skipped strings**. The Backing Track guitar voicing menu offers Standard, Shell (R-3-7), Drop 2, Drop 3, Triads (three strings), 7ths no 5th (three strings), Voice-led triads, Voice-led upper triads and Two-note chords (3rd and 7th). Comping rhythm names are the labels in `catalog.js` (for example "Strum: down-up eighths", "Charleston", "Four to the bar"). Every fingering below was checked by computing the pitch classes of each string and fret.

### Sequence A: Harmonic and stylistic (pop and rock toward jazz)

For a guitarist who knows open chords and strumming but has not played jazz. Each step adds one idea to the previous voicing: first more chord types in familiar shapes, then fewer strings, then fewer chord tones, then more colour.

#### G-A1 Open triads (major and minor)
- **Shapes:** CAGED open shapes; the chords of [pop changes for guitar](../lessons/pop-changes-guitar.qmd).
- **Voicings:** C `x32010` (3 on A3, 2 on D2, 1 on B1); G `320003` (2 on E3, 1 on A2, 3 on e3); D `xx0232` (1, 2 on e2, 3 on B3); Am `x02210` (2, 3, 1); Em `022000` (2, 3); E `022100` (2, 3, 1); A `x02220` (1, 2, 3); Dm `xx0231`; F (small) `xx3211` (3 on D3, 2 on G2, 1 across B and e).
- **Lesson:** A triad is root, 3rd and 5th. The guitar plays six strings, so the same three tones are doubled: C `x32010` is C-E-G-C-E, with the 3rd (E) on three strings. The doubled 3rd and open strings give the bright, ringing sound of folk and pop. The 3rd decides major or minor, and that is the only difference between E and Em.
- **Practice:** play C, Am, F, G (loop 1 of [four-chord loops](../lessons/four-chord-loops.qmd)) until the changes are in time with no gap.
- **Unlocks:** "Acoustic strum", "Straight-eighth rock", "Country train beat", "Boom-chick" (strumFolk, strumEights, strumQuarters, oompah).

#### G-A2 Fifths (power chords)
- **Shapes:** root and 5th, with an optional octave. No 3rd, so neither major nor minor.
- **Voicings:** E5 `022xxx` (1 barre on A2 and D2); A5 `x022xx`; G5 `355xxx` (1 on E3, 3 on A5, 4 on D5); C5 `x355xx` (1 on A3, 3 on D5, 4 on G5). The shape is the same on the E string and on the A string, so one fingering moves everywhere.
- **Lesson:** Without a 3rd the chord is neutral. It stays clear under distortion (the 3rds of two chords clash in a distorted sound) and works over any 3rd the bass or singer supplies. It is the first example of dropping chord tones, which every later step does.
- **Practice:** play a 12-bar blues in fifths, then add a 6th-above-the-5th wiggle (E5 to E6 to E5) on the beat.
- **Unlocks:** "Emo / pop-punk" (strumPunk), "Straight-eighth rock", eighth-note palm muting.

#### G-A3 Barre chords (E-shape and A-shape)
- **Shapes:** the CAGED E and A shapes made movable (the nut becomes the index finger).
- **Voicings:** F `133211` (1 barre, 2 on G2, 3 on A3, 4 on D3); Fm `133111` (3 on A3, 4 on D3); Bb `x13331` (1 barre, 3 flat across D-G-B); Bm `x24432` (1 barre at 2, 2 on B3, 3 on D4, 4 on G4); Cm `x35543` (1 barre at 3, 2 on B4, 3, 4).
- **Lesson:** Any open chord shape can move up if the nut is replaced by a barre. The root is under the barre: on string 6 for the E shape, on string 5 for the A shape. This gives all 12 major and minor chords from two shapes, and it is the step where the neck becomes a map instead of a list of chords. Physical detail is in G-B7 and G-B8.
- **Practice:** play the same four-chord loop in three keys, naming each root as you land on it.
- **Unlocks:** the same strums in every key, such as the barre chords in "Motown / soul" and "Dance pop".

#### G-A4 Sus and add9 colours
- **Shapes:** the open shapes with one finger moved or lifted.
- **Voicings:** Dsus4 `xx0233` (1 G2, 3 B3, 4 e3); Dsus2 `xx0230`; Asus2 `x02200`; Asus4 `x02230`; Cadd9 `x32030` (3 A3, 2 D2, 4 B3); Gadd9 `320203` (2 E3, 1 A2, 3 G2, 4 e3).
- **Lesson:** In sus chords the 3rd is replaced by the 2nd (sus2) or 4th (sus4). With no 3rd they are neither major nor minor and they lean toward resolving: Dsus4 pulls down to D (G to F#). Add9 keeps the 3rd and adds the 9th (the 2nd an octave up), which sounds sweeter, not tense. These are the colours of pop and 80s rock guitar and the first non-triad chords of jazz.
- **Practice:** D, Dsus4, D, Dsus2 over four bars; then C, Cadd9.
- **Unlocks:** "Strum: sixteenths" with hammer-on figures, "John Prine Fingerpicking", "Fingerpicking: Travis pattern" (prine, travis).

#### G-A5 Dominant 7th chords
- **Shapes:** CAGED shapes with the 7th added, then barre E7 and A7 shapes.
- **Voicings:** C7 `x32310` (3, 2, 4, 1); G7 `320001`; D7 `xx0212`; A7 `x02020`; E7 `020100`; B7 `x21202`; F7 `131211` (1 barre, 2 on G2, 3 on A3); Bb7 `x13131` (1 barre, 3 on D3, 4 on B3).
- **Lesson:** A dominant 7th chord is root, major 3rd, 5th and flat 7th. The 3rd and the flat 7th form a tritone, which wants to resolve: the 3rd goes up a half step to the root and the flat 7th down a half step to the 3rd of the next chord. That pull is what a dominant chord is, and it is the engine of the blues and of every jazz tune.
- **Practice:** play G7 to C and name which two notes move by one fret. See [blues for guitar](../lessons/blues-guitar.qmd) step 2.
- **Unlocks:** "Blues shuffle" (rhythm `shuffle`), "Country train beat", and 12-bar blues with I7, IV7 and V7.

#### G-A6 Minor 7th and major 7th
- **Shapes:** open and barre shapes with the 7th.
- **Voicings:** Am7 `x02010` (2 on D2, 1 on B1); Em7 `020000` (2 on A2) or `022030`; Dm7 `xx0211` (2 on G2, 1 across B and e); Cmaj7 `x32000`; Fmaj7 `xx3210`; Gmaj7 `320002`; Amaj7 `x02120`; Dmaj7 `xx0222`. Barre: Fm7 `131111` (1 barre, 3 on A3); Bbm7 `x13121`; Bbmaj7 `x13231`; Cmaj7 `x35453`.
- **Lesson:** Minor 7th is a minor triad plus a flat 7th, and sounds soft and open, not dark. Major 7th is a major triad plus a major 7th, one fret under the root, and sounds dreamy. Pop already uses both (Am7 and Cmaj7 in "Cadd9, Am7, Fadd9" in the pop-changes lesson). In jazz these chord types, with dominant 7ths, make up most of the music.
- **Practice:** Cmaj7, Am7, Dm7, G7 (I vi ii V), with open shapes, then in F with barre shapes.
- **Unlocks:** "Bossa nova" (bossa), "Ballad: half-broken" (ballBroken), "Charleston" at a slow tempo.

#### G-A7 Half-diminished and diminished 7th
- **Shapes:** four-string shapes on strings 5-2 (A D G B), moved by fret, with the root on string 5 (m7b5) or in any position (dim7).
- **Voicings:** Bm7b5 `x2323x` (1 A2, 2 G2, 3 D3, 4 B3); Am7b5 `5x554x` (1 B4, 3 flat across E5 and D5, 4 G5; mute A); Bdim7 `x2313x` (2 A2, 3 D3, 1 G1, 4 B3); Cdim7 `x3424x`.
- **Lesson:** Half-diminished (m7b5) is a minor 7th with a flat 5th: it is chord vii of a major key (Bm7b5 in C) and the ii chord of a minor key (see [minor changes](../lessons/minor-changes.qmd)). Diminished 7th stacks minor 3rds, so the shape repeats every three frets: Bdim7 `x2313x`, Ddim7 `x5646x` and so on are the same four notes. The F# diminished passing chord in the pop-changes guitar lesson is this chord.
- **Practice:** slide a dim7 shape up three frets at a time and listen to the four names of the same chord.
- **Unlocks:** passing-chord progressions in [turnarounds](../lessons/turnarounds.qmd), minor-key ii-V-i.

#### G-A8 Shell voicings (root, 3rd, 7th)
- **Shapes:** three notes, on strings 6-4-3 (root on 6) or 5-4-2 (root on 5). Also called "7ths, no 5th" on the Chord Library.
- **Voicings (C):** C7 `8x89xx` (1 E8, 2 D8, 4 G9) or `x3x35x` (2 A3 muting D, 3 G3, 4 B5); Cmaj7 `8x99xx` or `x3x45x`; Cm7 `8x88xx` or `x3x34x`. Blues with root on string 6: see [blues for guitar](../lessons/blues-guitar.qmd) steps 3 to 5.
- **Lesson:** The 3rd and 7th are the guide tones that say what kind of chord it is. The 5th is the least important tone, because the bass and the key already imply it, so it is dropped. Root, 3rd and 7th leave the chord thin enough to sit under a singer or a solo and let other players fill in the rest. This is how a rhythm guitarist works in a jazz band.
- **Practice:** play C7, F7, G7 in shells and watch the 3rd and 7th: they move one fret each time the chord changes.
- **Unlocks:** "Four to the bar" (Freddie Green style), "Charleston", "Reverse Charleston", the jazz styles of the Backing Track that use the JAZZV voicing (guitar `shell3`).

#### G-A9 Triads on the top strings
- **Shapes:** three-string triads on strings 3-2-1 (set `321`), root position and both inversions.
- **Voicings:** C `xxx553` (root position), `xxx988` (first inversion), `xxx010` (second inversion); F `xxx565` (second inversion); Am `xxx210` (A-C-E, root position).
- **Lesson:** Three strings, three notes, no doubling: this is the cleanest chord and the one that sounds best in a band with another guitar or a keyboard, because it takes only a small slice of the range. Each triad has three inversions, found by moving the lowest note to the top. The shapes are small and move without changing finger shape, so you can chain them with very little movement ("voice-led triads" in the Chord Library menu).
- **Practice:** play C, F, G with the least possible movement between them, choosing the inversion nearest the last chord.
- **Unlocks:** "Strum: sixteenths" on the top three strings (funk), "Every off-beat" (upbeats), "Off-beats" (reggae and ska), PLAINV-style comping.

#### G-A10 Drop 2 voicings
- **Shapes:** four-note seventh chords on strings 4-3-2-1 (set `4321`), also on 5-4-3-2 and 6-5-4-3. Take the close-position chord and drop its second note from the top down an octave.
- **Voicings (C chords):** Cmaj7 `xx2413` (E B C G, 2 on D2, 4 on G4, 1 on B1, 3 on e3); C7 `xx2313`; Cm7 `xx1313`; Dm7 `xx3535` (barre at 3 on D-B, 3 on G5, 4 on e5); G7 `xx3433` (barre at 3, 2 on G4).
- **Lesson:** In close position the four chord tones are packed within an octave. Dropping the second voice from the top by an octave opens the chord up so that it fits the fingers and still keeps a rich sound. The ii-V-I above, Dm7 `xx3535`, G7 `xx3433`, Cmaj7 `xx2413`, moves each voice by one fret or step. That smooth voice leading is what jazz guitar chords are for.
- **Practice:** ii-V-I in C with those three shapes, then the same shapes on strings 5-4-3-2 and moved to the key of F.
- **Unlocks:** the "Drop 2" guitar voicing style, "Bossa nova" and "Charleston" comping with real voice leading.

#### G-A11 Drop 3 voicings
- **Shapes:** root on string 6 (or 5), skip the next string, then three notes on strings 4-3-2 (or 3-2-1). Order low to high: root, 7th, 3rd, 5th.
- **Voicings:** Cmaj7 `8 x 9 9 8 x` (index barre at 8, 3 flat across D9 and G9) or `x3x453`; C7 `8 x 8 9 8 x`; Cm7 `8 x 8 8 8 x`; Dm7 `x5x565`; G7 `3x343x` (barre at 3, 3 on G4). A ii-V-I with a small move: Dm7 `x5x565`, G7 `3x343x`, Cmaj7 `x3x453`.
- **Lesson:** Drop 3 is a drop 2 with a lower root: you gain a bass note and lose a string, so the skipped string must be muted. The sound is bigger than a shell, still 4 notes. Guitarists use drop 2 and drop 3 together: drop 3 when the root is low and the chord needs weight, drop 2 when the chord goes higher and lighter.
- **Practice:** play the same ii-V-I once with drop 3 and once with drop 2 and hear how the voice leading is nearly identical.
- **Unlocks:** "Drop 3" guitar voicing style, slower ballad comping ("Ballad: syncopated").

#### G-A12 Extensions (9th, 13th)
- **Shapes:** 7th chords with a 9th or a 13th replacing or adding to a chord tone.
- **Voicings:** C9 `x3233x` (1 D2, 2 A3, 3 G3, 4 B3); C13 `8 x 8 9 10 x` (1 barre on E8 and D8, 3 G9, 4 B10).
- **Lesson:** The 9th is the 2nd note up an octave, the 11th is the 4th, the 13th is the 6th. A 9th or 13th gives a chord colour without changing its job. C9 still pulls to F, but sounds richer; C13 is the usual dominant chord of the blues band. A useful shortcut: an Em7 shape played over a C bass note is Cmaj9, because E-G-B-D are the 3rd, 5th, 7th and 9th of C.
- **Practice:** swap each dominant 7th in a 12-bar blues for a 9th or 13th and listen to the colour.
- **Unlocks:** "Funk", "Boogaloo stabs" (stabs), jazz-blues with extended dominant chords.

#### G-A13 Altered dominants
- **Shapes:** dominant 7th shapes with the 5th or 9th raised or lowered.
- **Voicings:** C7#9 `x3234x` (the "Hendrix chord", 1 D2, 2 A3, 3 G3, 4 B4); C7b9 `x3232x` (1 barre on D and B at 2, 3 on A3, 4 on G3); C7#5 `8 x 8 9 9 x` (1 barre on E8 and D8, 3 on G9, 4 on B9).
- **Lesson:** An altered dominant adds tension to the V chord before it resolves. The b9 and #9 pull down toward the 5th or 3rd of the chord of arrival, and the #5 pulls up toward its 3rd. They are short, high-energy chords used at the end of a phrase. The b9 shape `x3232x` also contains the notes E, B-flat and D-flat (C#) of an Edim7 chord over a C bass, which is why dim7 shapes work as 7b9 chords.
- **Practice:** in a C ii-V-I, replace the G7 with a G7#9, which is the C7#9 shape seven frets up (`x 10 9 10 11 x`), and notice the tension relax on Cmaj7.
- **Unlocks:** turnarounds and the last bar of a chorus in "Charleston" and "Four to the bar" jazz comping, bluesy endings.

### Sequence B: Physical (by fingering difficulty)

For a beginner. Each step adds one physical demand. For each, the line "easy because / hard because" names what to expect.

#### G-B1 One or two fingers on adjacent strings
- **Shapes:** Em, Em7, E7, Am7 and fifths.
- **Voicings:** Em `022000` (2 on A2, 3 on D2); Em7 `020000` (only 2 on A2); E5 `022xxx`; E7 `020100` (2 on A2, 1 on G1); Am7 `x02010` (2 on D2, 1 on B1).
- **Easy because:** one or two fingers, no stretch, all notes within one or two frets. **Hard because:** the fretting hand has to press down firmly enough and the strum has to land on the right strings.
- **Common problems:** the fingertips hurt at first and strings buzz when the finger is behind the fret instead of just before it; the tip of a finger mutes a neighbouring string.
- **Practice:** hold Em and play each string one at a time to hear each ring clear; 5 minutes a day builds calluses.
- **Unlocks:** "Strum: D, D-U, D, D-U" with two chords.

#### G-B2 Three fingers, compact shapes
- **Shapes:** Am, E, A, D, Dm.
- **Voicings:** Am `x02210` (2, 3, 1); E `022100` (2, 3, 1); A `x02220` (1, 2, 3 or a flat index); D `xx0232` (1 on G2, 2 on e2, 3 on B3); Dm `xx0231`.
- **Easy because:** the fingers stay together in one place and no string is crossed over. **Hard because:** three fingers must be on three strings together, and the A chord crowds three fingers into one fret (some players use a small index barre instead).
- **Common problems:** the 3rd finger touches the high e string and mutes it (D chord); A chord fingers collide; slow changes between E and A.
- **Practice:** switch Am to E (fingers 2 and 3 slide one string over together) as slowly as needed.
- **Unlocks:** the loops in [four-chord loops](../lessons/four-chord-loops.qmd) that use only these chords; "Boom-chick".

#### G-B3 Reach and cross-string: C, G, Cadd9
- **Shapes:** C, G, Cadd9, Gadd9.
- **Voicings:** C `x32010` (3 on A3, 2 on D2, 1 on B1); Cadd9 `x32030` (3, 2, 4 on B3); G `320003` (2, 1, 3) or `320033` (2, 1, 3, 4); Gadd9 `320203`.
- **Easy because:** all within the first 3 frets. **Hard because:** the fingers are spread diagonally over three frets (a stretch from the 1st finger to the 3rd on a different string), and G needs the 1st and 3rd fingers far apart across the neck, with strings in between left open.
- **Common problems:** the index finger on C touches the high e string and mutes it; the thumb stays too high on the neck, which shortens reach, so keep the thumb low behind the neck; the hand rolls too far forward.
- **Practice:** form C, lift all fingers together and place them again without looking (a shape-landing drill).
- **Unlocks:** the pop-changes loop C, Am, F, G.

#### G-B4 Four-finger dominant sevenths
- **Shapes:** open C7, G7, D7, B7, plus the Fmaj7.
- **Voicings:** C7 `x32310` (3, 2, 4 on G3, 1); G7 `320001` (3 on E3, 2, 1 on e1); D7 `xx0212` (2 on G2, 1 on B1, 3 on e2); B7 `x21202` (2 on A2, 1 on D1, 3 on G2, 4 on e2); Fmaj7 `xx3210` (3, 2, 1).
- **Easy because:** the shapes are each a small change from the earlier ones (G7 moves one finger from G). **Hard because:** four fingers are used at once (C7 and B7), including the weaker 4th finger, and B7 is an awkward stretched cluster.
- **Common problems:** the 4th finger does not reach the string squarely; changes C to C7 need a finger added from a long way off.
- **Practice:** play C, then add the 4th finger on G3 only (C7), and go back and forth without lifting the others.
- **Unlocks:** "Blues shuffle", 12-bar blues in open positions.

#### G-B5 Mini-barres on two or three strings
- **Shapes:** F, Dm7, Fmaj7 in a partial barre.
- **Voicings:** F `xx3211` (3 on D3, 2 on G2, 1 flat across B and e); Dm7 `xx0211` (2 on G2, 1 flat across B and e); Dm `xx0231` as a stepping stone.
- **Easy because:** only two or three strings are covered. **Hard because:** this is the first time one finger has to press two strings at once, and the first finger has to turn sideways.
- **Common problems:** the B string is muffled by the flat of the finger (roll the finger slightly onto its outside edge); the e string does not sound; the thumb is too far toward the front of the neck.
- **Practice:** hold only the 1st-finger flat across B and e at the first fret and pluck both strings, check that each rings; then add the others.
- **Unlocks:** the F chord in the pop loop; I V vi IV in every key reachable with the open C shape.

#### G-B6 Top-string triads (small shapes, no barre)
- **Shapes:** three-string triads on strings 3-2-1.
- **Voicings:** C `xxx010` (1 on B1), `xxx553` (1 on e3, 3 flat on G5 and B5) and `xxx988`; F `xxx565`; Am `xxx210`.
- **Easy because:** fewer notes and fewer fingers. **Hard because:** the lower strings must not sound, so the strum becomes a pick or finger pluck of three strings (or the lower strings are muted by the right hand's thumb), and the triad moves higher up the neck where frets are closer together.
- **Common problems:** open lower strings ring (use the fretting hand fingers lightly across them, or the right-hand palm); the unplayed strings are hit by the pick; pitch is high and thin.
- **Practice:** play C `xxx010`, `xxx553`, `xxx988` in a loop, picking strings 3-2-1 one at a time and then together.
- **Unlocks:** top-string funk and reggae chops ("Strum: sixteenths", "Off-beats").

#### G-B7 Full barre, E-shape (strength)
- **Shapes:** the E shape with the 1st finger across all six strings.
- **Voicings:** F `133211`; Fm `133111`; F7 `131211` (1 barre, 2 G2, 3 A3 for F7); Fm7 `131111`.
- **Easy because:** the shape is a familiar one (E) and the barre finger is one fixed shape. **Hard because:** one finger has to press six strings at once, the pressing force is high, and the hand tires quickly.
- **Common problems:** buzzing strings (put the barre just behind the fret, move the finger so that the bony side, not the soft pad, lies on the string), the string 2 or 3 does not sound (the slight gap in the finger), the elbow and thumb are in a poor position. Fm and F7 are slightly easier than F because one finger fewer is needed, but the barre is the same. Ask for a lower-action guitar, or a capo, to practice.
- **Practice:** play the barre alone, with no other fingers, until all six strings ring; hold 10 seconds, rest 20, repeat. Capo at fret 1 and play the open E shape, then replace the capo with the finger.
- **Unlocks:** the same chord strums in any key, "Motown / soul".

#### G-B8 Barre, A-shape (cramped fingers)
- **Shapes:** barre A shape, root on string 5.
- **Voicings:** Bb `x13331` (1 barre, 3 flat across D-G-B); Bm `x24432` (1 barre at 2, 2 on B3, 3 on D4, 4 on G4); Bbm7 `x13121` (1 barre, 3 on D3, 2 on B2); Bb7 `x13131` (1 barre, 3 on D3, 4 on B3); Bbmaj7 `x13231` (1 barre, 3 on D3, 2 on G2, 4 on B3).
- **Easy because:** the barre only covers five strings; the shapes are small. **Hard because:** three fingers have to fit into three adjacent strings in one fret space, and frets are narrower as you move up the neck (more cramped). Some players use the 3rd finger as a flat barre over D, G and B, which is the toughest part.
- **Common problems:** the B string is muted by the finger's flat on the barre (hear for the dead note); the high e string, the last in the barre, is quiet because the index finger bends there.
- **Practice:** try Bm `x24432` by laying down only the barre and the 3rd finger first.
- **Unlocks:** barre chords around the neck in rock and funk.

#### G-B9 Position changes (shifting the shape)
- **Shapes:** E-shape and A-shape barres moved up the neck.
- **Voicings:** F `133211` to G `355433` to A `577655`; Bm `x24432` to Cm `x35543` to Dm `x57765`; F# `244322`.
- **Easy because:** the shape does not change, only the position. **Hard because:** you have to find the new fret without looking, release enough pressure to slide but not so much that the barre makes an unwanted sound, and keep the rhythm going.
- **Common problems:** pausing between chords; landing a fret off; scraping noise on the slide (release a little pressure between chords, don't lift).
- **Practice:** slide the F barre up one fret at a time to G and back at a steady beat; use the dots at 3, 5, 7 and 9 as landmarks. Use the "common finger" approach: keep one finger on a shared string while the others move.
- **Unlocks:** twelve-bar blues with barre chords in any key, I-IV-V in A or G.

#### G-B10 Barre seventh and major seventh shapes (finger independence)
- **Shapes:** E-shape and A-shape barres with the 7th.
- **Voicings:** F7 `131211`; Fm7 `131111`; Bb7 `x13131` (1 barre, 3 on D3, 4 on B3); Bbm7 `x13121`; Bbmaj7 `x13231`; Cmaj7 `x35453` (1 barre on A3 and e3, 2 on B4, 3 on D5, 4 on G5).
- **Easy because:** the base is a barre you already know and the 7th removes a finger or leaves an open one (F7 and Fm7 have the easiest changes because you remove a finger). **Hard because:** the 4th finger is needed for the first time at the end of a barre; Cmaj7 `x35453` also demands a long reach with the 4th finger.
- **Common problems:** the 4th finger flattens other strings; the small finger lacks strength.
- **Practice:** hold a barre and tap the 4th finger one fret at a time on one string; the tapping shows the independence.
- **Unlocks:** "Bossa nova" chords in barre positions; slow ballad jazz.

#### G-B11 Muting and skipped strings: shells
- **Shapes:** three-note shells (R-7-3) with one or two strings left out.
- **Voicings:** C7 `8x89xx` (1 on E8, 2 on D8, 4 on G9; A string touched by index); Cmaj7 `8x99xx`; Cm7 `8x88xx`; and on the A string C7 `x3x35x` (2 A3 muting D, 3 G3, 4 B5), Cmaj7 `x3x45x`.
- **Easy because:** three notes, so only three fingers, and the shapes are movable. **Hard because:** the strings in between must not sound, so the skipped string is muted by the underside of the finger above it (touching but not pressing); and the right hand has to pluck only three strings, either with fingers or by a pick with careful aim.
- **Common problems:** the skipped string rings (the fretting finger above lies too lightly); the shape sounds tinny if it is struck too hard.
- **Practice:** pluck one string at a time, thumb on the bass, fingers on the others, and then play them together.
- **Unlocks:** "Four to the bar", "Charleston"; see [blues for guitar](../lessons/blues-guitar.qmd) steps 3 to 5.

#### G-B12 Drop 3 shapes (barre plus skipped string)
- **Shapes:** four notes on strings 6-4-3-2 and 5-3-2-1 (skip one string).
- **Voicings:** Cmaj7 `8 x 9 9 8 x`; C7 `8 x 8 9 8 x` (index barre across fret 8, G9 by the ring finger); Cm7 `8 x 8 8 8 x` (barre only); Dm7 `x5x565`; G7 `3x343x`; Cmaj7 `x3x453`.
- **Easy because:** the shapes use the same finger layout (a barre and one note). Cm7 is only a barre. **Hard because:** barre strength with a muted string in the middle; the skipped A string has to be damped, and the high position on the neck means a long reach or position shift.
- **Common problems:** the muted string rings (a flat barre sounds the A string; lift the barre slightly in the middle or damp with the right thumb); wrong-string strums.
- **Practice:** play Cm7 `8x888x` alone with a barre, then add the ring finger to form C7.
- **Unlocks:** the "Drop 3" guitar voicing style.

#### G-B13 Drop 2 and stretched extensions (position, stretch and independence)
- **Shapes:** four-note chords on the inner strings (set `4321`), then 9ths and 13ths.
- **Voicings:** Cmaj7 `xx2413` (2, 4, 1, 3); C7 `xx2313`; Dm7 `xx3535` (barre at 3, 3 on G5, 4 on e5); G7 `xx3433`; C9 `x3233x` (1 D2, 2 A3, 3 G3, 4 B3); C13 `8 x 8 9 10 x` (4 on B10); C7#9 `x3234x`; C7b9 `x3232x`.
- **Easy because:** only four strings and the shapes are close to each other. **Hard because:** the fingers are in unusual combinations (for example 2-4-1-3 for Cmaj7), a mini barre and a skipped string are combined, and several demand a finger stretch of three or four frets. They are the most demanding chords in the sequence.
- **Common problems:** pain in the hand after practising for too long; wrong finger numbers; avoiding the shape because it feels odd; the reach makes the shape lose pressure on one note.
- **Practice:** learn only one ii-V-I in drop 2 at a time, saying the finger numbers aloud, then move it up a fret.
- **Unlocks:** "Drop 2" voicing style, jazz comping ("Bossa nova", "Charleston"), altered dominants of G-A13.

### Where the two sequences line up

| Sequence A | Sequence B | Note |
|---|---|---|
| G-A1 Open triads | G-B1, G-B2, G-B3 | The same chords seen as one-, three- and reach-shapes. |
| G-A2 Fifths | G-B1 | Two fingers; the E5 shape is the easiest. |
| G-A3 Barre E/A shapes | G-B7, G-B8, G-B9 | A is the sound of barre chords, B is the strength and the slide. |
| G-A4 Sus/add9 | G-B2, G-B3 | One finger moved; B3 (Cadd9) and B2 (Dsus) supply the fingerings. |
| G-A5 Dominant 7th | G-B4, G-B10 | Open 7ths are B4; barre 7ths are B10. |
| G-A6 m7 and maj7 | G-B5, G-B10 | Mini-barre and barre versions. |
| G-A7 m7b5, dim7 | G-B10 (finger independence) | Four-note shapes on the inner strings, with the 4th finger. |
| G-A8 Shells | G-B11 | The first muting and skipped-string step. |
| G-A9 Top-string triads | G-B6 | In B this step comes early because it needs no stretch; in A it comes after shells because the voice leading needs the 7th chord first. |
| G-A10 Drop 2 | G-B13 | The hardest fingerings. |
| G-A11 Drop 3 | G-B12 | A barre with a skipped string. |
| G-A12, G-A13 Extensions, altered | G-B13 | Stretch and independence. |

The sequences differ most in the middle: musically a shell (G-A8) is simpler than a full 7th chord, but physically it needs muting (G-B11), which comes after barre chords. A learner can do G-A8 and G-B11 after G-B4 even without a barre, because shells on strings 6-4-3 need no barre, only a 4-finger stretch. Likewise, top-string triads (G-A9) are physically easy (G-B6) but musically more advanced than barre chords. Lessons that link to these steps should name the step ID (for example "see G-A8 and G-B11 for shells").

## Piano

**Conventions.** Middle C is C4. Notes are listed from the bottom up. LH and RH are the left and right hands. Fingers are 1 (thumb) to 5 (little finger); a fingering is given in the same bottom-to-top order as the notes, so LH 5-3-1 puts the little finger on the lowest note. "Span" is the distance from lowest to highest note in one hand, in semitones. Chord-tone numbers (R, 3, 5, 7, 9, 13) are counted from the chord's root. Voicing-style names follow the site: **Close position**, **Shell**, **Guide tones**, **Rootless A/B**, **Drop 2** (the piano list in the Chord Library keyboard mode, `voicings.js`). Anything the site has no style for yet is marked "no style yet". Comping names are the Backing Track's: *Long notes, Quarter-note chords, Ballad: rocking / half-broken / syncopated, Arpeggio up and down, Arpeggio low and high, Triplet chords (12/8), Charleston, Reverse Charleston, Boogaloo stabs, Bossa nova, Son montuno*; style names are *Piano ballad, Acoustic strum, Motown / soul, 12/8 doo-wop, Medium swing, Jazz ballad, Boogaloo, Bossa nova, Son montuno*. Existing lessons referred to: [pop changes for piano](../lessons/pop-changes-piano.qmd), [blues for piano](../lessons/blues-piano.qmd), [guide tones](../lessons/guide-tones.qmd), [four-chord loops](../lessons/four-chord-loops.qmd).

The two sequences use the same chords (C, Am, F, G for pop; Dm7, G7, Cmaj7 for the two-five-one) so a step in one can be played with the same notes as its partner in the other. IDs are `P-A1`...`P-A13` and `P-B1`...`P-B13`.

### Sequence A: from pop triads toward jazz (harmonic order)

For a pianist who plays pop and rock chords and has not yet met jazz voicings. Each step changes one thing from the one before: how many notes, which chord tones, or where they sit.

#### P-A1 Root-position triads over a bass root
- **Voicing:** Close position, root position; LH single root, RH triad.
- **Spelling (C - Am - F - G):**
  - C: LH C3 (5); RH C4-E4-G4 (1-3-5)
  - Am: LH A2 (5); RH A4-C5-E5 (1-3-5)
  - F: LH F2 (5); RH F4-A4-C5 (1-3-5)
  - G: LH G2 (5); RH G4-B4-D5 (1-3-5)
- **Lesson:** A triad is root, 3rd and 5th. The 3rd decides major or minor; the 5th adds weight. The left hand names the chord with its root, the right hand fills it in. This is the sound of most pop piano, and it is the starting point for everything else: every later voicing is a rearrangement of these three notes or an addition to them.
- **Practice:** Play the four chords with a steady beat in the RH while the LH holds the root. Notice that the RH leaps (C chord to A chord to F chord) each time. The next step removes the leaps.
- **Unlocks:** Piano ballad with *Long notes* or *Quarter-note chords*; Acoustic strum.

#### P-A2 Inversions and smooth voice leading
- **Voicing:** Close position, root position and inversions chosen to move least.
- **Spelling (C - Am - F - G, RH only, LH root as before):**
  - C: C4-E4-G4 (root position, 1-3-5)
  - Am: C4-E4-A4 (1st inversion, 1-2-5)
  - F: C4-F4-A4 (2nd inversion, 1-2-5)
  - G: B3-D4-G4 (1st inversion, 1-2-5)
  - back to C: C4-E4-G4 (B moves up to C, D up to E, G stays)
- **Lesson:** Inverting a triad moves its lowest note to the top, so the hand can stay in one place. The note C stays put through the first three chords (it is the root of C, the 3rd of Am and the 5th of F), and the other notes move by a step. Smooth voice leading means each note travels as little as possible, which sounds connected and calm. The bass root still names the chord.
- **Practice:** Play the loop with the RH eyes-closed after the first time; the hand should only change by one finger's width. Then try it in F and G.
- **Unlocks:** *Ballad: rocking*, *Ballad: half-broken*, *Arpeggio up and down* (the three notes of the inversion are the arpeggio).

#### P-A3 Sus and add9 colours
- **Voicing:** Close position with one note replaced or added: sus2, sus4, add9.
- **Spelling:**
  - Csus2: LH C3; RH C4-D4-G4 (1-2-5)
  - Csus4: LH C3; RH C4-F4-G4 (1-2-5)
  - Cadd9: LH C3; RH E4-G4-D5 (1-3-5), that is 3-5-9
  - Fadd9: LH F2; RH A4-C5-G5 (1-2-5), that is 3-5-9
  - Gsus4: LH G2; RH G4-C5-D5 (1-2-5), then G4-B4-D5 (1-3-5) to resolve
- **Lesson:** sus2 and sus4 replace the 3rd with the 2nd or 4th, so the chord is neither major nor minor and wants to settle. add9 keeps the 3rd and adds the 9th (the 2nd an octave up), which gives the shimmering open sound of pop ballads. Put the 9th above the 3rd, not next to the root, or it clashes.
- **Practice:** Play Gsus4 then G (C falls to B) and say aloud what moved. Then play C, Cadd9 by moving only one RH finger.
- **Unlocks:** Piano ballad, *Arpeggio low and high*, *Ballad: syncopated*; the Gsus2 and Cadd9 bars of [pop changes for piano](../lessons/pop-changes-piano.qmd).

#### P-A4 Bass note plus triad: slash chords and split hands
- **Voicing:** Triad in the RH over a bass note that is not the root (slash chord), LH single note or octave.
- **Spelling (C - G/B - Am - C/G - F - F#dim - G):**
  - C: LH C3; RH C4-E4-G4
  - G/B: LH B2; RH B3-D4-G4 (1-2-5)
  - Am: LH A2; RH C4-E4-A4 (1-2-5)
  - C/G: LH G2; RH C4-E4-G4 (1-3-5)
  - F: LH F2; RH C4-F4-A4 (1-2-5)
  - F#dim: LH F#2; RH C4-F#4-A4 (1-2-5)
  - G: LH G2; RH D4-G4-B4 (1-2-5)
  - F/G (a bonus chord): LH G2; RH F4-A4-C5, which is G with b7, 9 and 11, a G11 or G9sus4
- **Lesson:** The two hands now play separate jobs: the LH walks the bass line (C, B, A, G, F, F#, G) and the RH holds triads that barely change. A triad over a different bass note gives a new chord name and a new sound; the F triad over G is the first jazz-flavoured chord of this sequence, and an idea we will come back to in P-A13.
- **Practice:** Play only the bass line, then the RH chords, then together, slowly. Keep the RH quiet and the LH singing.
- **Unlocks:** The passing-chord bars of [pop changes](../lessons/pop-changes.qmd); *Dotted* bass with *Ballad: rocking*; Motown / soul with *Quarter-note chords*.

#### P-A5 Four-note close seventh chords
- **Voicing:** Close position, root position, with the 7th added (maj7, dominant 7, m7); LH root.
- **Spelling:**
  - Cmaj7: LH C3; RH C4-E4-G4-B4 (1-2-3-5)
  - C7: LH C3; RH C4-E4-G4-Bb4 (1-2-3-5)
  - Am7: LH A2; RH A3-C4-E4-G4 (1-2-3-5)
  - Dm7: LH D3; RH D4-F4-A4-C5 (1-2-3-5)
  - G7: LH G2; RH G4-B4-D5-F5 (1-2-3-5)
- **Lesson:** A seventh chord stacks a 3rd on the triad. The type of 7th is the type of chord: B (major 7th) is bright and floating, Bb (flat 7th) is bluesy and restless, and the minor chord's flat 7th is soft. Dominant sevenths contain the tritone between the 3rd and the 7th (B and F in G7), which wants to resolve.
- **Practice:** Play C, Cmaj7, C7, Cm7 in turn, changing one finger each time, and describe the colour each time.
- **Unlocks:** Medium swing with *Quarter-note chords* (Freddie Green feel); the 7th chords in [blues for piano](../lessons/blues-piano.qmd) section 2.

#### P-A6 Inverted sevenths: the two-five-one
- **Voicing:** Close position, inversions chosen for voice leading.
- **Spelling (Dm7 - G7 - Cmaj7, RH, LH roots D3 G2 C3):**
  - Dm7: F4-A4-C5-D5 (1-2-3-5)
  - G7: F4-G4-B4-D5 (1-2-3-5)
  - Cmaj7: E4-G4-B4-C5 (1-2-3-5)
- **Lesson:** F stays, A falls to G, C falls to B, D stays; then F falls to E, G and B stay, D falls to C. Almost nothing moves, and the 7th of each chord (C, then F) falls by a step to the 3rd of the next (B, then E). That falling-7th-to-3rd line is the engine of jazz harmony.
- **Practice:** Play the three chords as a loop in time; sing the top note's line (D, D, C). Move to F (Gm7 C7 Fmaj7) and Bb.
- **Unlocks:** Medium swing, *Charleston* comping, ii-V-I turnarounds.

#### P-A7 Guide tones: the 3rd and the 7th
- **Voicing:** Guide tones (3rd and 7th only), two notes in either order, over a bass root.
- **Spelling:**
  - Dm7: LH D2; RH C4-F4 (7-3, 1-3)
  - G7: LH G2; RH B3-F4 (3-7, 1-4)
  - Cmaj7: LH C3; RH B3-E4 (7-3, 1-3)
  - for contrast, the same chords with the 3rd below: Dm7 F4-C5, G7 B3-F4, Cmaj7 E4-B4
- **Lesson:** The 3rd and 7th are the only notes that tell major from minor and dominant from major-seventh, so two notes can stand for the whole chord. The root belongs to the bass player and the 5th adds little. Notice how the line moves: C to B and F to F, then F to E and B to B. This is the lesson in [guide tones](../lessons/guide-tones.qmd).
- **Practice:** Play the two-five-one in guide tones and then sing the lines. Add a top note of your own, any chord tone.
- **Unlocks:** *Charleston* and *Reverse Charleston* with little clutter; the first half of [blues for piano](../lessons/blues-piano.qmd) (sections 3 and 6).

#### P-A8 Shell voicings: 1-3-7 and 1-7-3
- **Voicing:** Shell (root, 3rd, 7th), played by the LH alone; RH free for melody.
- **Spelling (1-3-7):**
  - Dm7: D3-F3-C4 (LH 5-3-1)
  - G7: G2-B2-F3 (LH 5-3-1)
  - Cmaj7: C3-E3-B3 (LH 5-3-1)
- **Spelling (1-7-3), root and 7th in the LH, the 3rd on top:**
  - G7: G2-F3-B3 (LH 5-1 on G2-F3, B3 with the RH thumb, since the whole shape spans a tenth)
  - Cmaj7: C3-B3-E4 (LH 5-1 on C3-B3, RH thumb on E4)
- **Lesson:** A shell leaves out the 5th and puts the three essential tones of the chord in a compact shape. Putting the 7th next to the root (1-7-3) makes the 3rd the top note, which sounds more open and is the standard shape for a jazz LH; the 1-3-7 form is closer to blues piano. The root is there because there is no bass player.
- **Practice:** Play the two-five-one in the LH only, then add a melody in the RH. Alternate 1-3-7 and 1-7-3 so the hand barely moves.
- **Unlocks:** Solo piano, trio comping without a bass, the shell exercises in [blues for piano](../lessons/blues-piano.qmd) section 4.

#### P-A9 Rootless voicings A and B
- **Voicing:** Rootless A/B (the site's name). Form A has the 3rd at the bottom, form B has the 7th at the bottom.
- **Spelling:**
  - Dm7 A: F3-A3-C4-E4 (3-5-7-9, LH 5-3-2-1)
  - G7 B: F3-A3-B3-E4 (7-9-3-13, LH 5-3-2-1)
  - Cmaj7 A: E3-G3-B3-D4 (3-5-7-9, LH 5-3-2-1)
  - the other chain: Dm7 B C4-E4-F4-A4 (7-9-3-5); G7 A B3-E4-F4-A4 (3-13-7-9); Cmaj7 B B3-D4-E4-G4 (7-9-3-5)
- **Lesson:** With a bass player the root is already covered, so the left hand takes the 3rd, the 7th and two colour tones (the 9th, and the 13th on a dominant chord). The A and B forms alternate, so each chord is only a step or two from the last and the hand hardly moves. This is the left-hand sound of jazz piano from the 1950s on, and it is what the site's jazz styles use by default.
- **Practice:** Play the three chords in time with the metronome and then a bass line (D, G, C) in the other hand. Learn the chain that starts on A, then the one that starts on B.
- **Unlocks:** Jazz ballad with *Long notes*; Medium swing with *Charleston*; Bossa nova; Boogaloo with *Boogaloo stabs*.

#### P-A10 Drop 2 voicings
- **Voicing:** Drop 2: take a close four-note chord and move its second-highest note down an octave.
- **Spelling (two-five-one with the top line C - B - B):**
  - Dm7: A3-D4-F4-C5 (LH A3 D4 as 3-1; RH F4 C5 as 1-5)
  - G7: G3-D4-F4-B4 (LH G3 D4 as 5-1; RH F4 B4 as 1-5)
  - Cmaj7: G3-C4-E4-B4 (LH G3 C4 as 3-1; RH E4 B4 as 1-5)
- **Lesson:** Dm7 in close position is D4-F4-A4-C5; the second from the top is A, which drops to A3. The chord is now spread over about an octave and a half, which sounds fuller and more orchestral. A chord with the melody note on top can be drop-2'd to harmonise it. The root is back in the voicing, in the middle, as the second note.
- **Practice:** Take a simple melody (the first phrase of any tune) and play each melody note as the top of a drop 2 chord. Drop 3 (move the third from the top) is the same idea one voice lower.
- **Unlocks:** Block-chord melody (the Shearing and Bill Evans style), solo piano, *Long notes* under a vocal.

#### P-A11 Spread voicings: bass pair plus upper triad
- **Voicing:** Spread (no style yet): root-5th pair in the LH, a rootless triad of the chord's upper tones in the RH.
- **Spelling:**
  - Dm9: LH D2-A2 (5-1); RH F4-C5-E5 (1-3-5), that is 3-7-9
  - G13: LH G2-D3 (5-1); RH F4-B4-E5 (1-3-5), that is 7-3-13
  - Cmaj9: LH C2-G2 (5-1); RH E4-B4-D5 (1-3-5), that is 3-7-9
- **Lesson:** An open fifth in the low register is the cleanest bass sound a piano has, because it stays clear of the muddy middle. Above it, the RH plays a triad made from 3rd, 7th and 9th. That triad is a minor triad over the root (Em over C), which is why a chord with a seventh and ninth can sound so simple. The top line is the same smooth one as in P-A9.
- **Practice:** Hold the open fifth with the pedal, then play the RH chord on top of it. Learn the root-fifth pair as a pair, with no thinking.
- **Unlocks:** *Arpeggio low and high*, *Ballad: syncopated*, Jazz ballad, gospel and singer-songwriter ballads.

#### P-A12 Quartal voicings
- **Voicing:** Quartal: notes stacked in perfect fourths (no style yet).
- **Spelling:**
  - "So What" chord (Dm11): E3-A3-D4 (LH 5-3-1) and G4-B4 (RH 1-3); from the bottom, E-A-D-G-B: fourths, fourths, fourths, then a major 3rd
  - Quartal triad over D (Dm11, no 3rd): D4-G4-C5 (RH 1-3-5) with LH D2
  - Quartal triad over G (G7sus4): G2 in the LH; RH D4-G4-C5 (1-3-5), which resolves by moving C to B
  - The same shape moves up the keyboard in parallel: E4-A4-D5, F4-Bb4-Eb5
- **Lesson:** Fourths have no 3rd in them, so the chord is neither major nor minor; it sounds open, modern and modal. The "So What" voicing (Miles Davis, 1959) is the best-known case. Because every interval is the same, you can slide the shape up and down and it will always sound consistent.
- **Practice:** Play the "So What" chord, then the same chord a half step up, and repeat the A section. Then play the shape in the RH only.
- **Unlocks:** Modal jazz, the *Maiden Voyage* bass-and-drums feel in the site's "From tunes" group, Bossa nova.

#### P-A13 Upper-structure triads
- **Voicing:** Upper-structure triad (no style yet): a shell in the LH, a whole triad of a different major chord in the RH.
- **Spelling:**
  - G7(9, #11, 13): LH G2-B2-F3 (5-3-1); RH A4-C#5-E5 (1-3-5), an A major triad
  - G7(b5, b9): LH G2-B2-F3; RH Db4-F4-Ab4 (1-3-5), a Db major triad
  - Cmaj7(9, #11, 13): LH C3-E3-B3 (5-3-1); RH D4-F#4-A4 (1-3-5), a D major triad
- **Lesson:** The LH shell fixes the chord's name and type; the RH triad is built from the extensions above it, so one familiar shape gives an unfamiliar chord. D major over C is C major with a 9th, a #11th and a 13th, and A major over G7 is the same kind of colour on a dominant. You already know the idea from P-A4: a triad over a different bass note.
- **Practice:** Play G7 with a plain shell, then drop the A triad on top. Then the Db triad, and listen to how it pulls to C. Try the triad a whole step above any dominant chord.
- **Unlocks:** Jazz ballad endings, tags and turnarounds in [turnarounds](../lessons/turnarounds.qmd); reharmonising.

### Sequence B: by what the hand can do (physical order)

For a beginner whose hands are getting a progression of tasks: more notes, bigger spans, black keys, two hands, leaps, and independence. "Span" is the lowest to highest note in semitones.

#### P-B1 Five-finger position on white keys
- **Voicing:** none; single notes, one per finger.
- **Spelling:** RH C4-D4-E4-F4-G4 (1-2-3-4-5); LH C3-D3-E3-F3-G3 (5-4-3-2-1). Span 7.
- **Easy:** every key is white and every finger has one key; no stretching.
- **Hard:** fingers 4 and 5 are weaker and fingers 3 and 4 share tendons; most beginners collapse the 5th finger and hold the wrist too low.
- **Lesson:** Rest each finger over its key with a curved hand, as if holding a ball. The thumb sits on its side and the little finger on its tip.
- **Practice:** Play five notes up and down with a slow pulse and the same tone on every note. Do the LH and RH an octave apart.
- **Unlocks:** single-line LH roots; the bass notes in P-A1.

#### P-B2 White-key triads, one hand
- **Voicing:** Close position triads on white keys; RH 1-3-5, LH 5-3-1.
- **Spelling:** RH C4-E4-G4 (C), F4-A4-C5 (F), G4-B4-D5 (G), D4-F4-A4 (Dm), E4-G4-B4 (Em), A3-C4-E4 (Am). LH: C3-E3-G3 (5-3-1).
- **Easy:** the hand is the same shape, a fifth wide, as in P-B1, and only fingers 1, 3 and 5 are used, so no changes in hand position.
- **Hard:** the three notes must sound together and evenly. Fingers 3 and 5 are weaker than the thumb, so the bottom note comes out too loud and the top note is thin. The 3rd of major and minor chords is a different key, which is a notation reading problem, not a hand problem.
- **Lesson:** A triad is a hand shape. Learn the *shape* of major (3 white-key steps then 2) and minor (the other order) and move it around.
- **Practice:** Play the chord, release, and place the hand again without looking. Check your wrist is level.
- **Unlocks:** P-A1 on its own.

#### P-B3 Bass note plus triad: two hands together
- **Voicing:** Close position triad in the RH, root in the LH (LH 5, RH 1-3-5).
- **Spelling:** C: LH C3 (5); RH C4-E4-G4 (1-3-5). Then Am (A2; A4-C5-E5), F (F2; F4-A4-C5), G (G2; G4-B4-D5).
- **Easy:** the LH has one note, so it has only one job. The hands are in the same position on both sides of the body, and a chord change is just one hand shape moved a step.
- **Hard:** the two hands have to land together; the hand that is better at it leads and the other drags. Many beginners lift one hand before the other. The RH leaps by a fifth or a fourth between triads, which you cannot do while watching the notes.
- **Lesson:** This is your first two-hand coordination: learn the chord change as one move, not as two.
- **Practice:** Play C - Am - F - G in half notes. Close your eyes after one repeat. Count out loud.
- **Unlocks:** P-A1; Piano ballad with *Long notes*.

#### P-B4 Inversions and small hand shifts
- **Voicing:** Close position inversions, RH 1-2-5 for 1st and 2nd inversion, 1-3-5 for root position.
- **Spelling:** C4-E4-G4 (1-3-5), C4-E4-A4 (1-2-5), C4-F4-A4 (1-2-5), B3-D4-G4 (1-2-5) as in P-A2. Span 7 to 9.
- **Easy:** the hand moves by a step at a time instead of leaping, and the fingering has only two patterns to learn.
- **Hard:** the span is now 8 or 9 (a 6th), so the 2nd finger and 5th finger start to stretch and the thumb must stay relaxed. Do not squeeze the keys with finger 2 or the sound will be uneven. The 1-2-5 shape leaves a gap between fingers 2 and 5 that the beginner wants to fill with finger 3 or 4.
- **Lesson:** An inversion is the same triad, in a different hand shape. Sort out which finger takes the new note.
- **Practice:** Play root position, 1st, 2nd inversion up the keyboard on C, then on F and G.
- **Unlocks:** P-A2; smooth voice leading under pop songs.

#### P-B5 Black keys in triads
- **Voicing:** Close position triads that contain black keys.
- **Spelling:** D major D4-F#4-A4 (1-3-5); E major E4-G#4-B4 (1-3-5); A major A3-C#4-E4 (1-3-5); Bb major Bb3-D4-F4 (1-3-5); Eb major Eb4-G4-Bb4 (1-3-5).
- **Easy:** D, E and A major have the black key under finger 3, which is the longest finger and reaches it most easily. The hand stays close to the white keys.
- **Hard:** Bb and Eb major put a black key under the thumb or the little finger. The thumb is short and has to reach in over the keys; the little finger tends to flatten. You also have to read the key signature. The hand must come a little deeper into the keyboard (arm further back from the fallboard, forearm slightly turned) for the thumb's black key.
- **Lesson:** Black keys are higher and shorter; long fingers (2, 3, 4) reach them most easily, and short fingers (1, 5) like the white keys. Pick the fingering, then choose the key that suits the hand.
- **Practice:** Play D, A, E first, and only then Bb and Eb. Do the same with the LH.
- **Unlocks:** chords in keys other than C (the key strip on the lesson pages), [four-chord loops](../lessons/four-chord-loops.qmd) in every key.

#### P-B6 Two-note guide-tone shapes
- **Voicing:** Guide tones, 3rd and 7th (or 7th and 3rd), in the RH.
- **Spelling:** Dm7 C4-F4 (1-3); G7 B3-F4 (1-4, a tritone, span 6); Cmaj7 B3-E4 (1-3, span 5).
- **Easy:** only two notes, so the hand is always relaxed; the shape does not change except for the span, which is 5 to 6 semitones.
- **Hard:** the interval changes between chords (a 4th, a tritone, a 4th), so the finger distance varies and the thumb is on a different type of key. The tritone is a "wide third" and needs fingers 1-4 or 1-5. The black keys in other keys again.
- **Lesson:** Two notes are enough for a chord, and the hand can move as a pair. Practise the fingers as a group rather than one at a time.
- **Practice:** Play the pair and hold it, then change the pair while keeping the arm still. Play both hands, with the LH playing the root.
- **Unlocks:** P-A7; Charleston comping; blues guide tones.

#### P-B7 Four-note close sevenths in one hand
- **Voicing:** Close position sevenths, RH 1-2-3-5.
- **Spelling:** Cmaj7 C4-E4-G4-B4 (span 11); C7 C4-E4-G4-Bb4 (span 10); Am7 A3-C4-E4-G4 (span 10); Dm7 D4-F4-A4-C5 (span 10); G7 G4-B4-D5-F5 (span 10).
- **Easy:** all the notes are a third apart, so the fingers fall on alternate keys of a scale, and every fifth finger lands naturally on the top note.
- **Hard:** a span of 10 to 11 is a 7th, so the hand must open further and finger 5 must reach. Finger 4 is skipped, so the weakest link does not get used, but fingers 2 and 3 must stay curved and the thumb must not fall flat. The chord is more likely to be played unevenly.
- **Lesson:** Only the notes you can play evenly sound like a chord; if the hand hurts, drop the 5th finger's note or change to a closer shape (P-A6 inversions are narrower).
- **Practice:** Play the chord, then alternate between root position and the closer inversion. Keep the wrist level.
- **Unlocks:** P-A5 and P-A6; [blues for piano](../lessons/blues-piano.qmd) section 2.

#### P-B8 Shells in the left hand
- **Voicing:** Shell (root, 3rd, 7th).
- **Spelling:** Dm7 D3-F3-C4 (LH 5-3-1, span 10); G7 G2-B2-F3 (LH 5-3-1, span 10); Cmaj7 C3-E3-B3 (LH 5-3-1, span 11). The 1-7-3 form C3-B3-E4 has span 16, so play C3-B3 with the LH and E4 with the RH.
- **Easy:** three notes, one hand, and the LH can play it without any help from the RH. The 1-3-7 form is only a seventh wide, which is about the same as P-B7.
- **Hard:** the span is bigger than a triad and the left hand is weaker than the right. The 1-7-3 form is a tenth, too wide for many hands, so it is shared with the RH. Mistakes in the LH are heard more easily because the register is low.
- **Lesson:** A shell with an extra note in the RH is the same chord with a larger span. Check your thumb: it should rest on the key's front edge, not hang over.
- **Practice:** Play the shells slowly, listening for each note to sound. Move the shape down by a half step and ask the hand if it still works.
- **Unlocks:** P-A8; solo piano; the shells exercises in [blues for piano](../lessons/blues-piano.qmd).

#### P-B9 Rootless voicings: four fingers in the LH
- **Voicing:** Rootless A/B.
- **Spelling:** F major keys: Dm7 A F3-A3-C4-E4 (span 11); G7 B F3-A3-B3-E4 (span 9); Cmaj7 A E3-G3-B3-D4 (span 10). In F: Gm7 A Bb3-D4-F4-A4 (span 11); C7 B Bb3-D4-E4-A4 (span 9); Fmaj7 A A3-C4-E4-G4 (span 10). LH 5-3-2-1 throughout.
- **Easy:** the hand moves by a half step or whole step at a time, because the A and B forms alternate. The B forms are the narrowest (span 9) so they feel easy. In F, Bb is under the little finger, which can play a black key at the side without trouble.
- **Hard:** four fingers have to sound together, with a span of 9 to 11 semitones. The LH is weaker. You must know the forms in every key, and the black keys appear in different fingers in each key. The common mistake is forgetting which form comes next.
- **Lesson:** Practice one chain in one key until it is automatic (F is easy for the LH), then move to C and Bb.
- **Practice:** Do the chain slowly with only the LH, then add a bass line in the RH, then play the LH with the RH melody.
- **Unlocks:** P-A9; Jazz ballad, Bossa nova, Boogaloo.

#### P-B10 Leaps: bass note and chord
- **Voicing:** Close position triad in the LH after a bass note: a boom-chick or stride left hand.
- **Spelling:** C: bass C2 (5), then chord E3-G3-C4 (LH 5-3-1). F: bass F2 (5), then F3-A3-C4 (5-3-1). G: bass G2 (5), then D3-G3-B3 (5-3-1). The leap from bass to chord is 16 semitones from C2 to E3.
- **Easy:** the shapes are the same triads, with chords in the middle of the keyboard so no black-key difficulty is added.
- **Hard:** it is a hand jump of 1.5 octaves while still on the beat. The eyes want to follow the hand and the rhythm drops. The chord is struck by landing on it, so it is usually too loud or too late. The bass notes below C2 are muddy and the chords below C3 too.
- **Lesson:** Do not look at the keys: feel the bass note and feel the black keys on each side of the chord. Land without a hard stop: lift the hand in an arc and prepare the shape in the air.
- **Practice:** Play LH only: bass, chord, bass, chord, with a metronome at 60. Close your eyes when the landing is reliable.
- **Unlocks:** *Quarter-note chords* with a stride bass; the Freylekhs / polka style (*upbeats*); country and folk comping.

#### P-B11 Stacked fourths: a hand shape that moves
- **Voicing:** Quartal (no style yet).
- **Spelling:** LH E3-A3-D4 (5-3-1, span 10) with RH G4-B4 (1-3); RH triad D4-G4-C5 (1-3-5, span 10), then up one step E4-A4-D5, then F4-Bb4-Eb5.
- **Easy:** every interval is the same, so the hand has one shape that can be moved up and down the keyboard in parallel. The span is 10, like P-B7.
- **Hard:** every fourth is a different mixture of black and white keys, so the *same* shape feels completely different in each place. The fingers do not know where they are going. In the LH, the little finger and the thumb stretch.
- **Lesson:** Think of the hand, not the keys. Learn the physical shape first, then find where it lands on the keyboard.
- **Practice:** Slide the shape up by step from D to D an octave higher, once with each hand. Note which positions feel the best, and why.
- **Unlocks:** P-A12; modal jazz.

#### P-B12 Wide stretches: octaves, 9ths and 10ths
- **Voicing:** Spread / open: the LH plays the bass pair or a 10th.
- **Spelling:** octave C2-C3 (LH 5-1, span 12); open fifth plus 10th: C2-G2-E3 (LH 5-2-1, span 16, rolled); 10th C2-E3 (LH 5-1, span 16); 9th C2-D3 (LH 5-1, span 14).
- **Easy:** an octave is easy for most adult hands, and the 9th is possible with a firm wrist. The notes are all white keys.
- **Hard:** a tenth is more than most hands can play at once, and a tight hand will damage a joint. Roll the notes (bottom first) instead of squeezing. The thumb should reach the top note, not the forearm. In keys with black keys, the thumb has a different distance.
- **Lesson:** A small hand rolls the chord; a large one plays it together. Do not stretch beyond comfort. Never push the hand into pain.
- **Practice:** Roll C2-G2-E3 slowly and listen to the three notes become one. Then play the same thing with the pedal.
- **Unlocks:** P-A11; *Arpeggio low and high*, *Ballad: syncopated*, Piano ballad.

#### P-B13 Independence: a shell under a moving right hand
- **Voicing:** Upper-structure triad over a shell.
- **Spelling:** LH G2-B2-F3 (5-3-1) held; RH A4-C#5-E5 (1-3-5) played on the off-beats. Then LH Cmaj7 C3-E3-B3 with RH D4-F#4-A4.
- **Easy:** the LH is fixed, so only one hand has to change. The RH is still a plain triad.
- **Hard:** each hand has a different rhythm, and both are playing three notes. The beginner's habit is to play both hands on the same beat. The black keys in the RH triad (C#, F#) are new. The hands must also be mentally separate: the LH has a chord and the RH a different one.
- **Lesson:** Independence is a step-by-step skill: learn each hand separately, then two beats of each hand, then the whole bar.
- **Practice:** Play the LH on beat 1 and the RH on the "and" of 2, then on "and" of 4. Keep a steady foot tap.
- **Unlocks:** P-A13; Charleston; Reverse Charleston; Boogaloo stabs; Son montuno.

### Where the two sequences line up

| Physical (B) | Harmonic (A) | Note |
|---|---|---|
| P-B1, P-B2, P-B3 | P-A1 | The same notes, different reasons: the position, then the triad, then the two hands. |
| P-B4 | P-A2 | Inversions are both a harmonic and a hand-size decision. |
| P-B2 / P-B3 | P-A3 | sus and add9 use the same hand shapes as triads, with one finger moved. |
| P-B3 | P-A4 | A walking bass under still triads needs the two-hand coordination in P-B3. |
| P-B7 | P-A5, P-A6 | Four notes in one hand. |
| P-B6 | P-A7 | The two-note shapes are physically easier than P-B7, but harmonically they come later. Teach P-B6 before P-B7 if hand size is the issue. |
| P-B8 | P-A8 | Same step. |
| P-B9 | P-A9 | Same step. |
| P-B10, P-B12 | P-A10, P-A11 | Drop 2 and spread voicings are the "wide" voicings; both depend on leaps and stretches. |
| P-B11 | P-A12 | Same step. |
| P-B13 | P-A13 | Same step; also needed for all the comping rhythms in Sequence A. |
| P-B5 | any step | The black keys are not a topic in Sequence A. Work through each A step in F, Bb and G as well as C. |

The two sequences diverge in two places. Sequence A reaches guide tones (P-A7) before shells (P-A8) and shells before rootless (P-A9), and so does B, but A puts four-note close sevenths (P-A5) before guide tones, while B (P-B6) puts the two-note shapes first because they are easier. A learner with small hands can do P-B6 then P-B7. A learner who reads well can follow A in order.

  (one confirming burst at that level), hard cap -24 dBFS. On headphones it plays four bursts and stops at the cap. The test checks the
  cap holds.

  mic 40 dB louder than what was played). Now a match must have the sweep's shape (normalised correlation >= 0.3), be no more than 20 dB
  louder than what was played, and come back at the same delay (within 3 ms) on a second burst. Not yet tried on real speakers: the
  thresholds may need loosening in a reverberant room.
